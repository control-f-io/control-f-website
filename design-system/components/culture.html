Components

Culture

A band of photographs, each one making a claim about how the place works. It is the "Wie wir arbeiten" section of karriere.html and it is the only block in the system where a photograph is the argument rather than the illustration of one — every other picture on the site sits beside text that would stand without it. Here the sentence beside the frame is a caption for the frame, not the other way round.

One <ul> under a hairline contour, one <li> per claim, and the picture changing sides row by row. Everything the block decides — which side, which crop, which ratio — is stated on the row, so a row can be moved, removed or reordered without a rule elsewhere having to be told.

The band

Three rows, the arrangement that ships. Portrait frames take the narrow track and stand on the right; the landscape frame takes the wide track and leads.

The threshold is 56 rem asked of the band's own width, not the window's — so narrowing this browser folds the specimen below it into one column per row, picture then claim, exactly as it folds on a phone. The band is a container, which is why it also folds correctly when it is put inside a column narrower than the page.

  • Wir fassen die Hardware an, über die wir reden

    Telemetriedaten fangen nicht im Data Warehouse an, sondern am Sensor. Deshalb liegen zwischen den Laptops Platinen, Messgeräte und Klebeband.

  • Die Kaffeepause gehört zur Arbeit

    Die Küche ist der zweite Besprechungsraum. Vieles, wofür es sonst einen Termin gebraucht hätte, ist dort in zehn Minuten entschieden.

  • Zwei vor einem Bildschirm sind keine verlorene Stunde

    Bei uns zieht man jemanden dazu, statt sich allein durchzubeißen. Fragen kosten Minuten, falsche Annahmen kosten Wochen.

      <img class="cf-culture__photo" src="/assets/img/culture/hardware.jpg"
           alt="Zwei Kollegen am Laptop, davor eine aufgebaute Sensorplatine"
           width="1067" height="1600" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
    </figure>
    <div class="cf-culture__body">
      <h3 class="cf-culture__title">Wir fassen die Hardware an</h3>
      <p>Telemetriedaten fangen nicht im Data Warehouse an …</p>
    </div>
  </li>

  <li class="cf-culture__row cf-culture__row--wide">
    <figure class="cf-culture__figure" style="--ar:3 / 2"> … </figure>
    <div class="cf-culture__body"> … </div>
  </li>

</ul>
The figure is first in the source on every row, whichever side it is drawn on. --tall moves the picture to the second column with grid-column, not by reordering the markup. The picture is the subject of the claim beside it, so a reader on a screen reader or with CSS off meets it in that order down the whole band; only the drawing alternates.

The two frames

There are two rows and no third. The modifier names the frame's orientation, and the side the picture is drawn on follows from it — a portrait frame is narrow, so it takes the narrow track; a landscape frame is wide, so it takes the wide one. The alternation a reader sees down the band is a consequence of alternating the crop, not a separate decision to be kept in step by hand.

Why the tall row reaches back across the line for its rule. A border sits inside the box that declares it, so the same column boundary is a different pixel depending on which neighbour draws it: border-right in a wide row is the last pixel of track 1, border-left in a tall row would be the first pixel of track 2. Measured at 1440 on a 2× capture before this was fixed, the band's vertical hairline stood at x 847 down the wide rows and x 848 down the tall ones — four 1 px jogs from top to bottom, and a 2 px stagger between the pictures' facing edges. One pixel is the whole stroke here, so the eye reads the line as broken rather than as thin; it was reported in exactly those words, "the pictures are not aligned". The tall row therefore draws the rule where the wide row draws it, stated as a position rather than as a repeated track width, so it cannot drift from the 3fr / 2fr split above it. ::before and deliberately not a box-shadow: photography gives a photograph "no frame and no shadow", and a shadow on a figure is that sentence's subject however structural the intent. → scripts/check-band-rule-continuity.py
--ar and --pos are the row's two decisions. The ratio is on the figure because a band whose frames all had the same proportion would be a grid, not a band. --pos overrides the crop's anchor and is the escape hatch for a frame whose subject is not centred: a cover crop throws away a real part of a real picture, and which part it throws away is a decision worth writing down rather than inheriting. No shipping row states it today — all three frames are centred correctly — so it exists on the component and not yet in the page.

Accessibility

  • The band is a <ul> of <li>: the claims are a countable set, and the count on entry is what tells a reader how long the band is before they commit to it. role="list" is on it because list-style: none removes the role in Safari, and it must not also be load-bearing for the UA padding inside the contour — that is why the reset states margin and padding itself.
  • Every photograph carries a real alt, and this is the block where that is the rule rather than the exception. A portrait next to a name gets alt="" because the name is the alternative; here the picture is the evidence for the sentence beside it, so a reader who cannot see it is missing the argument and not a decoration. Each one describes what the frame shows and nothing it does not.
  • The <figure> carries no <figcaption>, and the title beside it is not one. A caption is bound to its figure by the element; the claim here is a heading with its own paragraph, sitting in a sibling cell, and it reads as the section's next point rather than as a legend. The figure element is the frame, and the alt is the picture's alternative — see the note below.
  • Each title is an h3 under the section's h2. Three headings for three claims is what a heading list is for, and unlike the team grid — where seven names would be seven headings with nothing under any of them — each of these has a paragraph under it that belongs to it.
  • No tab stop and none needed: nothing scrolls, and no cell holds a link. The band is read, not operated.
  • Contrast: the title is full ink on the page wash; the paragraph is --text-secondary, 5.9 : 1 on the same ground. No text is ever set over a photograph in this block, which is what keeps it out of the contrast problem every image-with-text-on-it band has.
  • Nothing moves, so prefers-reduced-motion has nothing to stop. Under forced colours the hairlines are borders and a background — the row divider and the wide row's rule survive as borders; the tall row's ::before is a filled box and is forced with the rest of the page.
  • width and height on every <img> so the intrinsic ratio reserves the box before the file lands, and loading="lazy" because the band sits low on its page.
A <figure> without a <figcaption> is a deliberate choice, not an unfinished one. The element earns its keep here as the frame — a self-contained piece of content the flow refers to — and the row's claim is deliberately not marked up as its caption: a figcaption would bind the sentence to the picture as its legend, and this band's sentences are points the section is making, each one strong enough to be a heading. Marking them as captions would take three headings out of the page's outline and put three legends in.

Countthree rows ship. The band is an argument, and a fourth claim is usually the first one restated
This is not the team grid and not the blog grid. All three are photographs in a ruled field, and the difference is what the picture is doing. The team grid shows who — the portrait is an identifier and its alt is empty because the name is beside it. The blog grid shows what to read — the picture is a link's target made visible. The culture band shows evidence — nothing in it is a link, and the photograph is the only thing in the row that cannot be replaced by typing the sentence more emphatically.

Do

  • Photograph the actual room, the actual people and the actual hardware — the band's whole argument is that it is not stock.
  • Alternate the frames, and let the crop decide the side rather than picking sides by hand.
  • State --ar on the figure and --pos on any frame whose subject is off-centre.
  • Write the title as a claim someone could disagree with. "Zwei vor einem Bildschirm sind keine verlorene Stunde" is a position; "Zusammenarbeit" is a label.
  • Give every frame a full alt. Here the picture is the evidence, so an empty one drops the argument.
  • Keep the band under .cf-section-header--flush, so one hairline does both jobs.

Don't

  • Never set text over the photograph. No overlay, no scrim, no title on the frame — the claim lives in its own cell and that is what keeps the band at AA without a gradient holding it there.
  • Don't reorder the markup to move the picture. grid-column moves the drawing; the source order is the reading order.
  • Don't give the figure a figcaption. The claim is a heading, and a caption would take it out of the page's outline.
  • Don't reach for border-left on a tall row's figure. It is the same rule one pixel over, and the band's hairline steps sideways at every row.
  • No frame, no shadow, no rounded corner on a photograph — corners are square everywhere but the logo pill, the nav pill and a round avatar.
  • Don't link a row. Nothing here goes anywhere; the register of open positions below it is what a reader acts on.
  • Don't grow the band past three or four rows. It is the section's argument, not a gallery.
