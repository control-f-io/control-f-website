Components

Line

A quantity over a domain. The plot draws a short series as objects standing in space and names its own ceiling at five values; past that ceiling the system had nothing. This is the other half of that argument — the same lattice, the same contour, the same direct labelling, drawn as a section rather than in space, because a line is not a body.

The figure

A frame, two hairlines, and one black line. The frame states where it starts and stops; the trace is drawn into it; three of the eleven values are printed, and the other eight are in the markup as text without being painted. No vertical rule, no tick, no legend — and no scale either, because a figure that prints the three numbers it is about is a figure with nothing left to estimate.

A second series

One line may be read against one other, and the second is the comparison — the plan, the year before, the target — never a peer. It is drawn broken: the 1-4 ghost, one rung down the presence ladder, which is how this system says “less present” everywhere else. It also carries the figure's one hue, and the dash is the half that is load-bearing: the rung is what a reader gets in forced colours, in greyscale and on a monochrome printer, and the tint is what makes the two lines separable at a glance in the crossing region where a duty cycle is hardest to read. One is the ceiling, and that is arithmetic. Two series is also the case that needs no key at all: each names itself at its own last point, which is direct labelling and beats a legend every time. Three is the next section.

Presence is a line type here and not a tint, and that is arithmetic before it is doctrine. The obvious way to draw a second series is to grey it, and grey fails at both ends at once. Against CF-Grau, the wash's worst end: --border-default composites to #9D9D9D and reads 1.74:1, grey 500 1.78:1, grey 400 2.02:1 — every grey light enough to read as a second series is under the 3:1 this system holds a contour to, which is a line the reader is invited to compare and cannot see. The one grey that clears the wash, grey 700 at 5.87:1, is 2.30:1 from the black it is supposed to differ from, and it is --text-secondary, the label ink: a trace in it reads as annotation rather than as data. The dash costs nothing by comparison — black is black at every width — and the 1-4 ghost is the same mark .cf-plot__cell and .cf-iso__ghost already carry for the same meaning. The hue on the ghost above is the one thing that clears both of those bars at once, and the next section is the measurement that says so — but it arrives on top of the dash and never in place of it, so removing every colour from this figure leaves it exactly as legible as it was before the colour existed.
The second series names itself in every key. Plan 2015, Plan 2016, … rather than an aria-label on the list or a legend beside it. Only one of those keys is painted, but all eleven are in the accessibility tree, so the two series read as two series to a screen reader without one word of the figure existing twice.

More than two, and the key

The four line types are the whole vocabulary a series can be drawn in here, so the ceiling is four traces and the practical one is three. But the ladder is ordinal — it says how present a thing is — so three series read honestly only if the three are ordinally related. One ordering fits it exactly: measured, planned, forecast. Present, one rung down, not here yet.

And this figure takes no hue at all, which is the ceiling showing rather than an omission. The page has room for exactly one coloured trace (the arithmetic is below), and the hue's meaning is “the thing the subject is read against”. With two series there is exactly one of those and the tint is unambiguous. With three there are two, and colouring one of them would say the other is a different kind of reference when the only difference between them is a rung. So the three-series figure is drawn in the vocabulary it has, and the moment a figure wants more, the answer is the one it always was: four frames.

The key is a convenience and never the mechanism. Every point in every series already carries its own name as text — Ist 2019, Plan 2019, Prognose 2019 — so the two series a reader cannot see are still two series to a screen reader with the key removed. That is also why the key sits above the frame rather than under the caption where legends usually end up: a reader who has to look below a figure to find out which line is which has already left the figure. It costs no vertical space, because .cf-line__bounds was spending that line anyway.
The figure above prints no values at all, and that is the trade the section is about. A value label is about 28 px of type and gap — a fixed height against a frame that is 320 px on a desktop and 136 px at 320 px wide — so at phone size it wants a fifth of the frame's whole range clear above its own node. In a multi-series figure that space usually belongs to another trace: measured here at 375 px before the set went quiet, the plan ran straight through the subject's opening 27 %, because the 13 points between them is 21 px there and the label is 28. So .cf-line__set--quiet keeps the keys, drops the printed values, and the numbers go in the caption, which has room for them. The accessibility tree still gets all twenty-one.
Two series may not print their keys at the same --t. Both would be centred on the same place under the frame and would land on top of each other. That is why the figure above reads its numbers off the solid line only and names the other two in the key — which is the trade the key exists to make. With two series, prefer the reverse: name each at its own last point and skip the key altogether, the way the previous section does.

One hue, and why there cannot be two

The brand has three accents besides lime, each with a full ramp, so a categorical set is not obviously unavailable — it was measured rather than assumed. The measurement came back with a smaller answer than “no” and a more useful one: this page has room for exactly one coloured trace, and that ceiling is a property of the page rather than of the palette.

The reason the obvious version of this arithmetic goes wrong is that a trace owes two debts at once, not one. It owes 3:1 against the page — whose worst end is CF-Grau, relative luminance 0.624 — and it owes 3:1 against the black trace it exists to differ from, which is Schwarz at 0. Solve both and the colour's own luminance is pinned into a band:

BarSolves to
3:1 off the wash's worst endY ≤ (0.624 + 0.05) / 3 − 0.05 = 0.175
3:1 off the black traceY ≥ 3 × 0.05 − 0.05 = 0.100
widest contrast available inside that band(0.175 + 0.05) / (0.100 + 0.05) = 1.50:1

Which settles the categorical question outright. Two colours that both clear the page and both clear the black line are under 1.5:1 from each other, and a 1 px stroke gives the eye almost no area to judge a hue on at the best of times. So a second hue is not something this brand happens to lack — growing the brand a categorical ramp would only produce more colours that fail each other. The band is 0.075 wide and it holds one step.

Of the twenty-eight steps in the four ramps, exactly one lands in it.

Everything light enough to carry a hue fails the page; everything dark enough to clear the page is a near-black that fails the trace. Violett 700 sits in the gap, at 5.68:1 on the wash's white end and 1.61:1 from grey 700 — so it is not --text-secondary either, which is the trap a grey trace falls into: it would read as annotation rather than as data. That is .cf-line__trace--hue, and there is deliberately no --line-hue property behind it. A knob would read as a choice, and there is no choice to make.

It rides with a rung and never instead of one. Nothing here may be encoded in colour alone, and this component has two reasons beyond the rule. In forced colours every trace is handed to CanvasText, so the hue is gone there and the dash is the whole of what a reader has — which is why the figure with the tint on it is exactly as legible with every colour stripped out. That hand-off is explicit and has to be: forced colours does not touch SVG stroke, so a tinted trace left alone comes through the mode untouched, which looks like the hue working and is the hue escaping. scripts/check-line-trace.py's RUNGS rule stands untouched on the other side: two traces in one figure may not share a rung, hue or no hue. What the colour buys is the one thing a dash on a hairline is worst at — telling two crossing lines apart at a glance, without stopping to inspect a duty cycle.
The hue goes on the reference and never on the subject. The measurement is the black line: “the contour is black and is the drawing” is older than this component and the trace is the contour. Tinting the comparison says the same thing the rung already says — this is not the measurement — so the two channels agree instead of competing, which is what redundancy is supposed to mean. And it is why a three-series figure takes no hue at all: with two references, one tint has to pick one, and the pick would claim a difference in kind that only a rung separates.

The scale

Everything above prints the values it is about, and the figure in More than two prints none — which leaves a reader with a shape, a frame and no way to put a number on anything between the ends. That is the case this is for. The frame gets subdivided, each subdivision carries its number, and the reader can read a value off anywhere along the domain.

It is a label rule, which is what makes it legal. Geometry admits three kinds of line and calls everything else decoration, and its third kind is “a mono label with the hairline running out of it to the far edge — the line and the word are one mark”. A step is that construction exactly: the number, a gap, the hairline to the frame's right edge. It is .cf-section-header, turned into the frame. The vertical rules stay forbidden for the reason they always were — a rule standing between two points carries no label at its end and closes nothing — so there is still no domain axis, and there still does not need to be. The domain is named at the points it is read at.

  <ol class="cf-line__set cf-line__set--quiet">…</ol>

The ends are not steps, and that is what keeps the scale from being a second copy of anything. The floor and the ceiling are already drawn — they are the frame's own two hairlines — and already named, by .cf-line__bounds, which stays required and still carries the unit. So the scale prints the subdivisions only, bare, and no figure says a number twice. A step at --v: 0 or --v: 1 would also put half its numerals outside the frame it is measuring.
A scale and a printed value are two answers to the same question, and a figure picks one. Print three numbers when three numbers are the message; draw a scale when the shape is the message and the reader wants to estimate anywhere along it — then let the set go --quiet, the way the figure above does. This is not a stylistic preference, it is the left edge: a step's number and a --first point's value are both flush left, and at 320 px the frame is 136 px tall, so quarter steps sit 34 px apart while a value label is 28 px. An opening value at --v: .12 and a step at .25 overlap there. Measured, not feared. A figure with a scale does not print its opening value — it has a scale, which is what the reader would have read that number off.
Same grey and same weight as the frame's own edges, deliberately. A subdivision is the same instrument at the same volume as the edges it lies between, and --border-default is already the quietest line the figure draws at 1.74:1 on the wash's worst end — a rung below that is not a fainter rule, it is no rule. What separates a subdivision from an edge here is not tint: an edge runs the full width and carries no number, a subdivision stops at one. That is the label-rule distinction drawn rather than asserted, and it is the distinction that survives forced colours, where both go dashed for the reason the frame already did.
It is aria-hidden, for the drawing's reason. The scale is the instrument and not the data: all thirteen values are already in the accessibility tree as text, and the extent is already there as .cf-line__bounds. Exposed, “75 50 25” would read as a fourth series sitting between the real ones. The alignment mechanism is font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums and nothing else — steps with the same digit count set to the same width, so every rule starts at one x without a wrapper element to align them in. A sensible step already has one; a step series that does not is a step series to reconsider.

A frame that does not start at zero

The case the .cf-line__bounds rule exists for. Six quarters of availability between 99.2 % and 99.8 % drawn over a zero baseline is six identical points along the top edge — a true chart that shows nothing. Drawn over a stated floor it is the only picture of the work.

This is the figure that could lie, so it is the figure that declares itself. A column may not do this — it is a body standing on the ground, and cutting it lies about its volume — but a trace stands on nothing and its frame is a free choice. A tight frame is not dishonest; the same 0.6 points of availability are worth a chart. An undeclared one is, because the reader has no way to know the floor moved. Print the bounds on every figure, including the ones where the floor is zero, so that the one figure where it is not looks like the others rather than like an exception nobody notices.

Small multiples

The answer to categorical data, and the reason this component needs no palette. Four series that are four kinds of thing rather than four rungs of one thing do not go in one frame — not here, where the ladder is ordinal, and not anywhere, where four crossing lines is a figure a reader untangles instead of reading. They go in four frames on one scale.

No new component, and that is the point. It is .tiles tiles--fit with --tile set — the layout primitive base.css already ships — and one .cf-line per cell. Each figure keeps max-width: var(--line-max) and simply takes the cell it is given, so the 2:1 frame holds at every column count the row wraps to.
The repeated bounds line is not noise; it is the proof. Four identical Rahmen 0 – 100 % lines is how a reader knows the four panels share a scale, and it is the only thing that could tell them. Small multiples on four different frames are four charts that look like a comparison and are not one — the failure mode this arrangement exists to avoid, reintroduced by the thing that looks like tidying it up.

How it is constructed

Nothing here is a new shape either. The frame is the 2:1 tile, the anchor is the annotation's lattice cell, the dash is the 1-4 ghost, the numbers are the treatment .cf-stat gives every figure in the system, and the contour is the contour.

A line is not a body, and that is the first decision. Everything the manual draws in isometry is an object with faces and volume — a cube, a stack, a solid of revolution. A trace has neither: it encodes a position at each point of a domain and says nothing about extent. Drawing it in space would claim a body where there is none, and it would cost exactly what the plot measured it costs — both ground axes slope 26.57° on screen, so a series marching along either one subtracts a unit of drawn height per step, and over the eleven points above that is more than the whole climb. So the line is a section: orthographic, 90°, the fourth of the four angles and the one that page calls the default for layout edges. The lattice has not gone anywhere — every point the figure is read at stands on a cell — but the trace between those cells is flat, because that is what it is.
The trace's angle is the one angle in this system nobody chose. Geometry says every diagonal on the site is one of four values. A trace cannot be: its slope is the quotient of two numbers that arrived from somewhere else. Quantising it to 26.57° / 45° / 63.43° would be falsifying data to fit a brand — the same class of failure as the receding row the plot was rebuilt to remove, and worse, because that one only flattened the picture where this one would change the numbers. The frame is the brand's; the line inside it is the data's.
Which leaves the frame's ratio carrying the steepness, so it is fixed. 2:1 — the lattice tile's own ratio, the same 200 × 100 bounding box Geometry derives from a 200 px square. The literature would tune it per dataset, banking the median segment toward 45°, and that is a real perceptual result; this system still does not, because steepness is the thing a reader takes off a line. Two line charts in one document with two aspect ratios cannot be compared and nothing on the page tells the reader they differ. --line-ratio exists so that a document can choose once, from the three ratios the brand owns — 2, 1 and 0.5, the frame diagonals at 26.57°, 45° and 63.43° — and never per figure.
The floor is not assumed to be zero, and this is the other half of the body/trace distinction. A column is a body standing on the ground, so its floor must be zero: cutting it lies about its volume, which is why the plot has no baseline other than zero and says so. A trace stands on nothing, so its frame may start anywhere — and the moment that is true the frame has to say where it starts. That is what .cf-line__bounds is, and it is why it is required rather than optional. A tight frame is not dishonest; an undeclared one is.
The drawing is a normalised square that is stretched, on purpose. viewBox="0 0 100 100" with preserveAspectRatio="none", so x is --t × 100 and y is (1 − --v) × 100 and nothing anywhere converts between the drawing and the labels. Distorting the box is correct here rather than tolerated: the aspect ratio belongs to the frame, not to the data, and stretching the data space is precisely what changing --line-ratio means. It costs the drawing nothing — the only geometry inside is polylines, whose stroke is non-scaling and therefore 1 px on screen at every ratio and every width, and every round mark in the figure is HTML sitting on top of the SVG rather than in it.
No vertical lines at all, which is the table's argument reused: a rule between two points would be neither an edge, nor a division, nor a label rule, and Geometry calls a line that is none of the three decoration. So there is no domain axis and no tick: the domain is named where it is read, which is the same direct labelling the plot uses instead of a legend.

The value scale is the one exception, and it is one because it passes the same test rather than because it was wanted badly enough. A step is a number with a hairline running out of it to the far edge, which is that page's third kind exactly — the mark .cf-section-header already is. It stays optional for the reason the original argument was right about: an axis exists to let a reader estimate a value, so a figure that prints the values that matter has nothing left to estimate and gets none. The scale is for the other figure, the one whose message is a shape.
The one seam, and what closes it. The polyline's points and the list's --t / --v are the same numbers written twice, which is the thing this component could not design away: SVG cannot read a custom property off an <li>, and the CSS construction that avoids the second copy — a chain of rotated hairlines sized with hypot() and turned with atan2() — has no fallback that is still a chart on an engine without CSS trigonometry. So the copy stays and scripts/check-line-trace.py holds the two halves to each other: every point's (--t, --v) must land on its trace's vertex of the same index, the counts must match, the domain must run 0 → 1, and it must not go backwards. Drift here is invisible in the way this whole suite exists for — a label a few pixels off its own line looks like a label.

What makes it readable

Same four rules as the plot, and one this figure adds because it is the figure that has more values than labels.

RuleWhy
The text is the data, and there is no second copy of it Each <li> carries its key and its value as real text, so the figure reaches the accessibility tree as an ordered list of “2015 12 %”, “2016 15 %”, … including the points that are not painted. The drawing adds nothing the text does not already say, which is why it is aria-hidden. No visually hidden table, no duplicated markup.
A point is data by default and a mark only when asked The plot prints every value because it has at most five. A line exists precisely when there are more values than a reader can be handed, and eleven numbers strung along a trace is a figure nobody reads. So the default is quiet and the painted point is the opt-in — which means the eye gets three numbers and the screen reader still gets eleven. A whole series can go --quiet the same way, and the count is the same: twenty-one values in the tree, none of them painted, all of them there.
The frame declares itself Because a trace's floor may be anything, a reader cannot assume zero, and a chart whose frame is not stated can be made to say almost anything by choosing one. .cf-line__bounds is the sentence that closes that, and it is required.
Nothing is encoded in colour alone Two series differ by line type first, and the caption names which is which in words. A figure may tint one reference trace Violett 700 — the single hue the page has room for — but the tint always arrives on top of a rung and never in place of one, so stripping every colour out leaves the figure exactly as legible. Forced colours does precisely that, which is the test rather than the worry. There is no lime in this figure at all: a point that has to be lit gets an annotation, which already owns the lit rung and the lime budget that goes with it.
The caption states the message, not the mechanics “Zehn Jahre Rollout, aber die Hälfte davon fällt in die drei Jahre nach der Umstellung des Zählerparks 2022” — the claim, then what it rests on. A caption that only names the axes has left the reader to do the interpretation the figure was drawn to save them.

Contrast: the trace is --border-strong (black) and the frame — its two edges and every scale step — is --border-default, both on the page wash. A tinted trace is Violett 700, 3.65:1 on CF-Grau and 3.70:1 from the black it is read against, which is the whole of why it is that step and not another. Values are --text-primary; keys, the bounds and the scale's numbers are --text-secondary, 5.9:1 on CF-Grau — never --text-muted, which is 2.0:1 there and is the mistake documented on Colour. In forced colours the frame's hairlines and the scale's are redrawn dashed, because that mode maps --border-default and --border-strong onto one ink and would otherwise hand the frame the trace's own weight — and every trace is given CanvasText outright, because that mode does not force SVG stroke and a Schwarz trace on a dark palette is a figure with its measurement missing.

The draw

The trace arrives along its own domain as the figure does, scrubbed from scroll position rather than played on a timer — the same posture as .cf-plot and .cf-iso (→ Motion). One registered number, --line-draw, drives a clip travelling left to right across the frame.

A wipe and not a dash, and this is the one place the component parts company with .cf-iso__trace. That trace draws with pathLength="1" and stroke-dashoffset, and pays for it by giving up non-scaling-stroke — under it the dash is measured in screen pixels while pathLength normalises in user units, so the draw stalls at the render scale. Its own note records what that cost: a weight that has to be authored per object and still ran from 0.38 to 1.71 CSS px across the tree. A chart cannot pay that. The trace is the datum, and a grey 0.5 px line at 2.5:1 on a phone is a datum the reader does not have.

It is also the better reading of the motion, which is the part worth keeping even if the arithmetic had gone the other way. A dash draw advances at constant arc length, so a steep year takes longer to draw than a flat one. A wipe advances at constant domain: every year takes the same scroll, and the line arrives at the rate time passed.
Only the trace moves, and every fallback is the finished chart. The frame, the bounds, the nodes and every printed number are drawn at once and stay drawn — they are the instrument, and a figure whose readable content waits for a timeline is a figure some readers never read. No support for view timelines, prefers-reduced-motion: reduce and print all leave --line-draw at its registered 1. The range also finishes inside the first third of the figure's cover range, so a line near the bottom of a short page reaches its last year rather than stopping short of it.

Do and don't

DoDon't
Reach for it when the domain is continuous and the shape is the point — a rollout, a load curve, a backlog. Use it for categories. Five things that are not a sequence are a plot, or a table; a line between them draws a change that did not happen.
Use it above five values. Below five, the plot is the better figure and says so. Go past about forty. The trace stops being a shape a reader can hold and starts being texture, and eleven points is already more than the printed numbers.
Print three or four values: the two ends and the moment the caption is about. Print all of them, or print none. The first is a figure nobody reads; the second leaves the reader estimating against a frame that has no scale.
State the frame, every time, even when the floor is zero. Leave .cf-line__bounds off because “it's obviously a percentage”. The one figure that needs it most looks exactly like the one that does not.
Use the ladder for series that are ordinally related — measured, planned, forecast — and say in the caption what each is. Use it for peers. Presence is not a category: “Strom, Gas, Wasser, Wärme” drawn as four rungs claims an order those four do not have. Four frames, not four dashes.
Name two series at their own last points and skip the key. Print keys from two series at the same --t. Both are centred on one place under the frame and land on top of each other — which is the collision the key exists to avoid.
Choose --line-ratio once for a document. Tune it per figure to make a climb look steeper. Two frames at two ratios in one document cannot be compared, and nothing tells the reader they differ.
Read a point where nothing else crosses it. Mark a point the other trace runs through. It is the one collision the geometry cannot prevent — a number that has a dotted line drawn across it is a number nobody trusts — and it is why the paired figure above prints three values and not four.
Give a printed value a fifth of the frame clear above its node, or let the series go --quiet and put the number in the caption. Print a value into another trace. The label's height is fixed and the frame's is not, so a gap that is generous on a desktop is a collision on a phone — and it is the phone that nobody checks.
Draw a scale when the message is the shape, and let the set go --quiet under it. Add a scale to a figure that already prints its values. They are two answers to the same question — and at 320 px the opening value and the first step land on the same 28 px of the left edge.
Step the scale in equal digit counts — 25/50/75, 20/40/60/80 — so the rules start at one x. Step it across a digit boundary, or step the ends. .cf-line__bounds names the floor and the ceiling and the frame already draws them.
Tint the reference, once, on top of its rung, when two lines cross and the dash is hard to follow. Tint the subject, tint two traces, or tint anything in a figure with three series. The page has room for one hue and its meaning is “not the measurement”; a second is under 1.5:1 from the first.
Keep the data clear of the floor and the ceiling. Let the trace run along the frame's own edge. It reads as the instrument, not the measurement, and the clip takes the top half of the stroke with it.

Still open

  • The manual's own statistics plate is still not in the curated source slice. assets/source/manual/ holds 22 plates and none of them is the statistics and diagram plate the full archive is known to contain. This page is therefore derived from the parts of the language that are documented — the lattice, the four angles, the four line types, the presence ladder, the annotation's anchor — exactly as the plot was, and with the same standing instruction: check it against that plate when the full archive is to hand, and if the two disagree, the plate wins.
  • Colour is answered and closed. One hue, and why there cannot be two is the whole of it, and the answer turned out to be a number rather than a no: a trace that owes 3:1 to the page and 3:1 to the black line it differs from is pinned to a luminance band 0.075 wide, and the widest contrast available inside that band is 1.50:1. So a figure may carry one coloured trace and never two, and that ceiling would survive the brand growing a categorical ramp — the second colour would fail the first, not the page. Violett 700 is the one step in the palette that lands in the band. It arrives as .cf-line__trace--hue, a modifier beside the rungs and never instead of them, which is the shape this entry predicted before it was measured.
  • No area under the trace, and no stack. The brand draws and does not fill, so a filled area would be the first surface in the system that is neither a material nor a face of an object. A band — a range, a confidence interval — is the version of this that is worth attempting, and the honest way in is probably two traces and a hatch rather than a wash. Not attempted.
  • Nothing is interactive. No hover readout, no crosshair, no tooltip. That is a deliberate stop rather than an omission: the component's whole premise is that the values a reader needs are printed, and a figure that hides its numbers behind a pointer has no answer for the reader who does not have one. The subdivision field is where this system keeps the argument for handing a reader control of a figure.
  • Gaps in the series are undefined. A missing year would have to break the polyline into two subpaths, and the check that holds the trace to the list assumes one vertex per point in order. Both are solvable; nothing needs it yet.
