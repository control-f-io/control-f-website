Components

Line

A quantity over a domain. The plot draws a short series as objects standing in space and names its own ceiling at five values; past that ceiling the system had nothing. This is the other half of that argument — the same lattice, the same contour, the same direct labelling, drawn as a section rather than in space, because a line is not a body.

The figure

A frame, two hairlines, and one black line. The frame states where it starts and stops; the trace is drawn into it; three of the eleven values are printed, and the other eight are in the markup as text without being painted. No axis, no ticks, no gridlines, no legend. 

<figure class="cf-line">

  <!-- required: where the frame starts and stops. The floor is not assumed -->
  <p class="cf-line__bounds">Rahmen 0 – 100 %</p>

  <div class="cf-line__frame">

    <!-- the drawing: a normalised square. x = --t x 100, y = (1 - --v) x 100 -->
    <svg class="cf-line__draw" viewBox="0 0 100 100" preserveAspectRatio="none" aria-hidden="true">
      <polyline class="cf-line__trace" points="0,88 10,85 … 100,16"/>
    </svg>

    <!-- the data. Every value is here; three of them are painted -->
    <ol class="cf-line__set">

      <li class="cf-line__point cf-line__point--read cf-line__point--first" style="--t:0;--v:.12">
        <span class="cf-line__key">2015</span><span class="cf-line__val">12 %</span>
        <span class="cf-line__node"></span>
      </li>

      <!-- a point nobody reads off the figure: no node, no paint, still data -->
      <li class="cf-line__point" style="--t:.1;--v:.15">
        <span class="cf-line__key">2016</span><span class="cf-line__val">15 %</span>
      </li>
      …
    </ol>
  </div>

  <figcaption class="cf-line__caption">Anteil der Netzanschlusspunkte…</figcaption>
</figure>

A second series

One line may be read against one other, and the second is always the comparison — the plan, the year before, the target — never a peer. It is drawn black and broken: the 1-4 ghost, one rung down the presence ladder, which is how this system says “less present” everywhere else. A third line is a legend, and this component has none.

Presence is a line type here, never a tint, and that is arithmetic before it is doctrine. The obvious way to draw a second series is to grey it, and a grey hairline on the page wash lands under the 3:1 this system holds its contours to — a series the reader is invited to compare and cannot see. The dash costs nothing: black is black at every width, and the 1-4 ghost is the same mark .cf-plot__cell and .cf-iso__ghost already carry for the same meaning.
The second series names itself in every key. Plan 2015, Plan 2016, … rather than an aria-label on the list or a legend beside it. Only one of those keys is painted, but all eleven are in the accessibility tree, so the two series read as two series to a screen reader without one word of the figure existing twice.

How it is constructed

Nothing here is a new shape either. The frame is the 2:1 tile, the anchor is the annotation's lattice cell, the dash is the 1-4 ghost, the numbers are the treatment .cf-stat gives every figure in the system, and the contour is the contour.

A line is not a body, and that is the first decision. Everything the manual draws in isometry is an object with faces and volume — a cube, a stack, a solid of revolution. A trace has neither: it encodes a position at each point of a domain and says nothing about extent. Drawing it in space would claim a body where there is none, and it would cost exactly what the plot measured it costs — both ground axes slope 26.57° on screen, so a series marching along either one subtracts a unit of drawn height per step, and over the eleven points above that is more than the whole climb. So the line is a section: orthographic, 90°, the fourth of the four angles and the one that page calls the default for layout edges. The lattice has not gone anywhere — every point the figure is read at stands on a cell — but the trace between those cells is flat, because that is what it is.
The trace's angle is the one angle in this system nobody chose. Geometry says every diagonal on the site is one of four values. A trace cannot be: its slope is the quotient of two numbers that arrived from somewhere else. Quantising it to 26.57° / 45° / 63.43° would be falsifying data to fit a brand — the same class of failure as the receding row the plot was rebuilt to remove, and worse, because that one only flattened the picture where this one would change the numbers. The frame is the brand's; the line inside it is the data's.
Which leaves the frame's ratio carrying the steepness, so it is fixed. 2:1 — the lattice tile's own ratio, the same 200 × 100 bounding box Geometry derives from a 200 px square. The literature would tune it per dataset, banking the median segment toward 45°, and that is a real perceptual result; this system still does not, because steepness is the thing a reader takes off a line. Two line charts in one document with two aspect ratios cannot be compared and nothing on the page tells the reader they differ. --line-ratio exists so that a document can choose once, from the three ratios the brand owns — 2, 1 and 0.5, the frame diagonals at 26.57°, 45° and 63.43° — and never per figure.
The floor is not assumed to be zero, and this is the other half of the body/trace distinction. A column is a body standing on the ground, so its floor must be zero: cutting it lies about its volume, which is why the plot has no baseline other than zero and says so. A trace stands on nothing, so its frame may start anywhere — and the moment that is true the frame has to say where it starts. That is what .cf-line__bounds is, and it is why it is required rather than optional. A tight frame is not dishonest; an undeclared one is.
The drawing is a normalised square that is stretched, on purpose. viewBox="0 0 100 100" with preserveAspectRatio="none", so x is --t × 100 and y is (1 − --v) × 100 and nothing anywhere converts between the drawing and the labels. Distorting the box is correct here rather than tolerated: the aspect ratio belongs to the frame, not to the data, and stretching the data space is precisely what changing --line-ratio means. It costs the drawing nothing — the only geometry inside is polylines, whose stroke is non-scaling and therefore 1 px on screen at every ratio and every width, and every round mark in the figure is HTML sitting on top of the SVG rather than in it.
No vertical lines at all, which is the table's argument reused: a rule between two points would be neither an edge, nor a division, nor a label rule, and Geometry calls a line that is none of the three decoration. No gridlines, no ticks, no axis. The domain is named where it is read, which is the same direct labelling the plot uses instead of a legend — and the reason neither figure needs one: an axis exists to let you estimate a value, and the values that matter are printed.
The one seam, and what closes it. The polyline's points and the list's --t / --v are the same numbers written twice, which is the thing this component could not design away: SVG cannot read a custom property off an <li>, and the CSS construction that avoids the second copy — a chain of rotated hairlines sized with hypot() and turned with atan2() — has no fallback that is still a chart on an engine without CSS trigonometry. So the copy stays and scripts/check-line-trace.py holds the two halves to each other: every point's (--t, --v) must land on its trace's vertex of the same index, the counts must match, the domain must run 0 → 1, and it must not go backwards. Drift here is invisible in the way this whole suite exists for — a label a few pixels off its own line looks like a label.

What makes it readable

Same four rules as the plot, and one this figure adds because it is the figure that has more values than labels.

RuleWhy
The text is the data, and there is no second copy of it Each <li> carries its key and its value as real text, so the figure reaches the accessibility tree as an ordered list of “2015 12 %”, “2016 15 %”, … including the points that are not painted. The drawing adds nothing the text does not already say, which is why it is aria-hidden. No visually hidden table, no duplicated markup.
A point is data by default and a mark only when asked The plot prints every value because it has at most five. A line exists precisely when there are more values than a reader can be handed, and eleven numbers strung along a trace is a figure nobody reads. So the default is quiet and the painted point is the opt-in — which means the eye gets three numbers and the screen reader still gets eleven.
The frame declares itself Because a trace's floor may be anything, a reader cannot assume zero, and a chart whose frame is not stated can be made to say almost anything by choosing one. .cf-line__bounds is the sentence that closes that, and it is required.
Nothing is encoded in colour The two series differ by line type, not by tint, and the caption names which is which in words. There is no lime in this figure at all: a point that has to be lit gets an annotation, which already owns the lit rung and the lime budget that goes with it.
The caption states the message, not the mechanics “Zehn Jahre Rollout, aber die Hälfte davon fällt in die drei Jahre nach der Umstellung des Zählerparks 2022” — the claim, then what it rests on. A caption that only names the axes has left the reader to do the interpretation the figure was drawn to save them.

Contrast: the trace is --border-strong (black) and the frame is --border-default, both on the page wash. Values are --text-primary; keys and the bounds are --text-secondary, 5.9:1 on CF-Grau — never --text-muted, which is 2.0:1 there and is the mistake documented on Colour. In forced colours the frame's two hairlines are redrawn dashed, because that mode maps --border-default and --border-strong onto one ink and would otherwise hand the frame the trace's own weight.

The draw

The trace arrives along its own domain as the figure does, scrubbed from scroll position rather than played on a timer — the same posture as .cf-plot and .cf-iso (→ Motion). One registered number, --line-draw, drives a clip travelling left to right across the frame.

A wipe and not a dash, and this is the one place the component parts company with .cf-iso__trace. That trace draws with pathLength="1" and stroke-dashoffset, and pays for it by giving up non-scaling-stroke — under it the dash is measured in screen pixels while pathLength normalises in user units, so the draw stalls at the render scale. Its own note records what that cost: a weight that has to be authored per object and still ran from 0.38 to 1.71 CSS px across the tree. A chart cannot pay that. The trace is the datum, and a grey 0.5 px line at 2.5:1 on a phone is a datum the reader does not have.

It is also the better reading of the motion, which is the part worth keeping even if the arithmetic had gone the other way. A dash draw advances at constant arc length, so a steep year takes longer to draw than a flat one. A wipe advances at constant domain: every year takes the same scroll, and the line arrives at the rate time passed.
Only the trace moves, and every fallback is the finished chart. The frame, the bounds, the nodes and every printed number are drawn at once and stay drawn — they are the instrument, and a figure whose readable content waits for a timeline is a figure some readers never read. No support for view timelines, prefers-reduced-motion: reduce and print all leave --line-draw at its registered 1. The range also finishes inside the first third of the figure's cover range, so a line near the bottom of a short page reaches its last year rather than stopping short of it.

Do and don't

DoDon't
Reach for it when the domain is continuous and the shape is the point — a rollout, a load curve, a backlog. Use it for categories. Five things that are not a sequence are a plot, or a table; a line between them draws a change that did not happen.
Use it above five values. Below five, the plot is the better figure and says so. Go past about forty. The trace stops being a shape a reader can hold and starts being texture, and eleven points is already more than the printed numbers.
Print three or four values: the two ends and the moment the caption is about. Print all of them, or print none. The first is a figure nobody reads; the second leaves the reader estimating against a frame that has no scale.
State the frame, every time, even when the floor is zero. Leave .cf-line__bounds off because “it's obviously a percentage”. The one figure that needs it most looks exactly like the one that does not.
Draw at most one comparison, and say in the caption what it is. Draw a third line, or use the ghost to mean “category B”. Presence is not a category, and this component has no legend to explain one.
Choose --line-ratio once for a document. Tune it per figure to make a climb look steeper. Two frames at two ratios in one document cannot be compared, and nothing tells the reader they differ.
Read a point where nothing else crosses it. Mark a point the other trace runs through. It is the one collision the geometry cannot prevent — a number that has a dotted line drawn across it is a number nobody trusts — and it is why the paired figure above prints three values and not four.
Keep the data clear of the floor and the ceiling. Let the trace run along the frame's own edge. It reads as the instrument, not the measurement, and the clip takes the top half of the stroke with it.

Still open

  • The manual's own statistics plate is still not in the curated source slice. assets/source/manual/ holds 22 plates and none of them is the statistics and diagram plate the full archive is known to contain. This page is therefore derived from the parts of the language that are documented — the lattice, the four angles, the four line types, the presence ladder, the annotation's anchor — exactly as the plot was, and with the same standing instruction: check it against that plate when the full archive is to hand, and if the two disagree, the plate wins.
  • No area under the trace, and no stack. The brand draws and does not fill, so a filled area would be the first surface in the system that is neither a material nor a face of an object. A band — a range, a confidence interval — is the version of this that is worth attempting, and the honest way in is probably two traces and a hatch rather than a wash. Not attempted.
  • Nothing is interactive. No hover readout, no crosshair, no tooltip. That is a deliberate stop rather than an omission: the component's whole premise is that the values a reader needs are printed, and a figure that hides its numbers behind a pointer has no answer for the reader who does not have one. The subdivision field is where this system keeps the argument for handing a reader control of a figure.
  • Gaps in the series are undefined. A missing year would have to break the polyline into two subpaths, and the check that holds the trace to the list assumes one vertex per point in order. Both are solvable; nothing needs it yet.
