Components

Block

Several series, compared category by category. The plot can draw one series and says so in its own Still open; this is the figure that draws three. It is the same argument in section — flat, orthographic, at the 90° angle the line already took — and the whole of what that projection buys is here: a second and third series, a value axis, and the one legend in this system.

The block chart

Three series across five categories, on a scale, in a frame. Every part is borrowed: the frame and the scale are .cf-line's, the 1:1 of block to air is the plot's 4u pitch written as a fraction, adjacent blocks share their contour the way the pie's neighbouring shares share a cut, and the faces are the brand's one ramp with its source swapped.

How it is constructed

Nothing here is a new shape either. The frame and its subdivisions are the line's, the pitch is the plot's, the shared contour is the pie's, the faces are the one ramp the brand has, and the numbers are the treatment .cf-stat gives every figure in the system. What is new is the projection, and everything else follows from it.

The block is the plot in section, and that is what buys the second series. A column in space spends both ground axes on the projection: one series marches along a lattice vector and a second would have to be stepped a cell back, behind the first, where it is occluded by exactly the thing it is being compared with. .cf-plot's own Still open says as much and declines it. Drawn orthographically at 90° — the fourth of the four angles, and the projection .cf-line already argued for — both screen axes are free: one carries the categories, the other the series. The figure gives up the body to get the comparison, which is the right trade here and the wrong one for a single number a reader is meant to remember. That is what the plot stays for.
The chart stands up, and the scale is the reason. The block chart most people picture lies on its side: categories as rows, value axis along the bottom. It is the one orientation this system cannot draw. The subdivisions of a horizontal value axis are vertical rules, and Geometry admits three kinds of line and calls the rest decoration — its third kind is “a mono label with the hairline running out of it to the far edge”, which a vertical rule between two points cannot be, because it carries no label at its end and closes nothing. Lying the chart down would cost it its axis. So the value axis is the vertical one, the blocks stand on the floor, the categories are named under the frame, and long category names are handled by fewer categories rather than by turning the figure. It is the same finding the pie made when it refused the ground plane, one figure along.
Each block is a CSS box, and that reverses the plot's decision on the same question. The plot draws in SVG because its columns are scaled — scaleY(--plot-h) on a shaft — and a 1 px CSS border inside a scaled box is scaled with it, while an SVG stroke under vector-effect: non-scaling-stroke is not. Nothing here is scaled: a block's height is a percentage of a frame, resolved at layout, so its contour is 1 px at every value, every width and every zoom without being asked. The section is the projection where the cheap medium is also the correct one — and it is what lets the faces below be written in oklab instead of carrying a hand-computed waypoint the way every lit face in an SVG has to.
The pitch is a fraction because the categories share the domain. In the plot the pitch is a length — 2u of column and 2u of air, five columns four units apart is a width — because a plot is an object you can measure. Here the frame is given and the categories divide it, so the plot's 1:1 has to survive a changing count, and the only form that does is a share. --block-fill: 0.5 is 2u of blocks and 2u of air at five categories, and it is still 1:1 at eleven.
The scale's numerals need ground of their own, and that is the one measure the line does not have to carry. .cf-line__scale sets its numbers inside the frame at the left and nothing else is ever there, because a trace is a hairline: it crosses a numeral as one more hairline and both stay legible. A block is a surface, and the first category stands exactly where the numbers are. Measured on the nine-category figure at a 700 px column before --block-gutter existed: the first block covered both digits of all three steps, so the figure had a scale a reader could not read. The set starts one gutter in; the numbers keep the line's position, and the rule runs the frame's whole width from behind them, past the blocks, to the far edge — which is the label rule drawn slightly more literally than the line draws it, not a different mark.
Adjacent blocks share their contour. One hairline everywhere rather than a doubled seam at every join — .cf-pie's rule for the cuts between neighbouring shares, and .cf-blog-grid's for the rules between its cells. It holds only where both blocks reach: above the shorter one the taller one's edge stands alone, which is what the drawing means.

One ramp, four sources

Colour states the family in one line: “There is only one gradient in this brand: lime falling away to CF-Grau, with Glas where the two meet.” A categorical figure needs more than one face and may not invent a second family, so it does what .cf-plot__col--fell already did for its one cool face — keeps the ramp and swaps the source. Lime stays the light and stays the one thing --lit spends; the other three are surfaces the light has not reached, which is what the palette already says they are.

SeriesThe rampWhere it comes from
first · no modifier Glas → CF-Grau #cf-pie-ground transposed onto a linear axis: “the bloom's own ramp without its lime leg, renormalised over the band”. Its job there is to say this is a surface, which is exactly what an uncoded block is.
--sky Sky → CF-Grau The same construction with the source swapped. Second in the spectrum's cool half.
--violett Violett → CF-Grau The same again. Third, and last: the palette has three chromatic colours that are not the light.
--lit lime → Glas → CF-Grau The family's own ramp, nothing swapped — .material-rake at 180°, Glas at the near rake. Emphasis, never a category, at most one per figure.

The order is the spectrum's own. --spectrum-stops runs lime, Weiß, Glas, Sky, Violett, so Glas → Sky → Violett is the cool half read in the order the brand already reads it in, and a series set drawn in that order is drawn ordinally rather than arbitrarily. Use it that way: three years, three stages, three grades. Three unrelated things given three hues is a legend pretending to be a scale.

No Glas stop in the middle of the other two, and that is measured. The obvious construction is the near rake with its source swapped — <hue> 0 %, Glas 32 %, CF-Grau 100 % — which is what the plot's violett cap draws, and on one face it is right. On a set of faces it is fatal. The leg from Violett to Glas turns 102° of hue, so under the arc it owes a waypoint at its polar midpoint, and that colour is #74C1E6 — Sky's own hue, at very nearly Sky's own chroma. The Violett series would pass through the Sky series on its way down, in a figure whose entire premise is that the two are different things. So a series ramp is a falloff and not a turn: source to CF-Grau, straight, never leaving its own hue. It owes no waypoint for the reason check-gradient-family.py gives — CF-Grau is achromatic, there is no chord to fall short of, and oklab's straight line is already the path.
Hue on a face is legal where hue on a hairline is not, and the arithmetic is already in the system. .cf-line measured the palette for a categorical stroke set and found none: Glas, Sky and Violett 500 come to 1.21, 1.49 and 2.24:1 on the wash, and the 800 steps that clear it are near-blacks 1.36 and 1.20:1 apart from each other — “a 1 px stroke has no area to judge a hue on”. The plot reached the same conclusion from the other side and moved its own direction mark off the contour and onto the face. A block is nothing but face. The 3:1 a UI shape owes is carried by the black contour round it, exactly as the plot's lime cap carries it, and the fill is free to be a hue.
The axis points down because the light comes from above. The source sits on the block's head, where a cap would be if this were a column, and falls away to CF-Grau at the floor. 180° is --angle-square turned a quarter — the same square angle the whole system is drawn on — and it is the choice .material-bloom--top already makes: “a surface lit from above has its light where the light comes from”.
The ramp is sized to the block, not to the frame. One light over the whole frame, with each block a window onto it, is the prettier construction and it makes the top of a short block grey — which is the series' own colour, lost, on exactly the blocks that have least of it to show. Sizing the ramp to the element keeps every block's head at its series' full hue, which is what the legend is naming. It is Colour's own rule: “the ramp is sized to the element and not to the page”.
Four ramps, four literal declarations, and that is about being checked. check-gradient-family.py resolves custom properties last-wins across the three stylesheets. Composed as one --block-ramp restated per modifier — which is how .material-rake is built, and correct there — all four gradients would expand to whichever ramp was declared last, and three of the four would pass the gate without ever being read by it. Written out, each one is checked. The @supports twin costs a second block and puts all four on the family's own path: measured, the worst divergence between the sRGB chord and the oklab line is 0.045 on lime → Glas, 0.006 on Violett → CF-Grau, 0.004 on Sky and 0.002 on Glas. Only the first is over the 0.0015 tokens.css treats as the point where the two paths part; one @supports block is cheaper than a rule about which faces are on the path and which are not.

The legend, and why this figure gets one

Every other figure in this system refuses a legend and each states the same reason. The plot: “that is a legend, in a component that has no legend, carried by a colour a third of readers cannot separate from its neighbours.” The pie: “a legend is the specific failure a pie is usually an example of.” Both are right, and both objections are about colour rather than about legends. So the block keeps the legend and takes the colour out of it.

What identifies a series here is where it stands in its group. The series run left to right inside every category in the order the legend lists them, and that order is geometry: it survives a monochrome screen, every colour vision deficiency, a forced palette, a printer that drops background images, and a photocopier. The hue is redundant reinforcement of a fact the figure has already stated twice — by position, and by the series name every block carries in the accessibility tree.

That is also what lets a block be lit. A lime block in position two is still in position two, so the plot's one-light-per-figure emphasis costs the legend nothing — which it would if hue were what the legend was made of. In the figure at the top the lit block is Bremen's 2025, and the caption names it in words, so nothing is encoded in a colour anywhere in the figure.
A one-series figure has no legend and must not grow one. There is nothing to tell apart, the categories are named under the frame, and a key naming one thing is a key that exists to be decorative. Below the plot's five-value ceiling, use the plot; above it, a block chart with no modifiers on any block and no __head legend at all.
Nine categories, and the keys are abbreviated rather than turned. The category axis has one line of mono per key and no more, because the alternative — rotating the keys — puts type at an angle the brand does not have and makes the figure taller by however long the longest name is. Nine three-letter keys fit a 375 px screen. Nine full site names do not, at any angle, and the answer to that is fewer categories or two figures, not smaller type.

The scale, and the floor that cannot move

The scale is .cf-line__scale — the same label rule, the same grey, the same refusal to print the ends. The floor and the ceiling are already drawn, as the frame's own two hairlines, and already named, by .cf-block__bounds. So the scale prints the subdivisions only, and no figure says a number twice.

What differs from the line is the floor. .cf-line__bounds states both ends because a trace stands on nothing: a tight frame is a legitimate choice there and an undeclared one is the dishonest case. A block stands on the floor, and its length is the quantity — so a floor anywhere but zero multiplies every comparison in the figure by a number the reader cannot see. Two blocks at 88 and 84 on a frame from 80 to 90 are drawn 2:1. The floor is therefore not a decision, which is why __bounds carries the ceiling and the unit and does not offer to carry a floor.

The blocks stand in front of the scale, and the line's trace runs behind it. That inversion is what a solid face requires: a trace is a hairline and the rules may pass beneath it without either being obscured, but a rule drawn across the front of a block is a rule inside the object. So __scale comes before __set in the markup, the blocks occlude it where they stand, and it runs on either side of them — which is exactly what the drawing means.
A printed number is a fixed height and the frame is not, so the frame's own height had to be found in the block's units. This is .cf-line__val's finding with a different denominator. The number needs its own line plus the gap — 0.75rem × 1.3 over --space-2, about 27.6 px — and that is the same 27.6 px whether the frame is 320 px tall on a desktop or 163 px at 375 px wide, where it is a seventh of the range. Measured before the clamp: the 88 % on the second figure crossed its own ceiling hairline by 4 px at 375 px, with the frame's top rule running through the numerals. The fix needed the frame's height, which the label cannot see — except that a percentage on it resolves against the block, and the block is --block-h of the frame. So 100% / var(--block-hz) is one frame height, expressed in the block's own units, and the clamp is the line's clamp with that substitution: no new token, no measured length, and it follows the frame at every width. --block-hz is the same number with zero taken out of it, because a division by zero would be invalid at computed-value time and drop the whole declaration rather than merely misplace it.
A clamped number lands on its own block's head, and every face clears it. Above --v: 0.9 at a 163 px frame the label has nowhere above the block to go, so it parks flush under the ceiling — which puts black mono over the block's own source colour. Measured: --text-primary on lime is 18.5:1, on Glas 16.4:1, on Sky 9.0:1 and on Violett 6.0:1, so the worst face in the set clears 4.5:1 with room to spare. That is not luck; it is what a palette of four light surfaces is.
A scale and a printed value are two answers to one question, and this figure has already picked. .cf-block__val is cropped by default: it stays in the accessibility tree at full strength and paints nothing. Fifteen numerals over five categories is not a chart, and the reader has a scale, which is what they would have read those numbers off. --read prints one, for the block the caption is about. This is .cf-line__set--quiet's rule, arrived at the same way and for the same reason — and it is the opposite of the plot's, which prints every value because it has no axis.

Parts of a whole, per category

The pie answers what one thing is made of. Nothing answered whether five things are made of the same thing, and five pies in a row is the figure that asks a reader to compare angles across five rotations — the comparison the pie's own header shows cannot survive even one squash. .cf-block--stacked is that figure: the series stack instead of standing side by side, bottom to top in legend order, and the shared contour turns with them. 

<figure class="cf-block cf-block--stacked">
  …
  <!-- --v is now the SEGMENT's share of the ceiling, not the running total.
       52 + 24 + 12 = 88, which is this category's whole -->
  <ol class="cf-block__bars">
    <li class="cf-block__bar" style="--v:.52">…</li>
    <li class="cf-block__bar cf-block__bar--sky" style="--v:.24">…</li>
    <li class="cf-block__bar cf-block__bar--violett" style="--v:.12">…</li>
  </ol>
--v changes meaning here, and the caption has to know it. In a grouped figure --v is the value against the ceiling. In a stack it is that segment's share of the ceiling, and the segments of one category sum to its total. A stack whose segments are authored as running totals draws every segment too tall and looks entirely plausible — the only thing that gives it away is arithmetic nobody does on a picture. It is the same class of fault as a plot's --v divided by the wrong ceiling, and the same defence: say in the caption what the parts are parts of.
Turning the axis costs one declaration, and getting it wrong draws equal thirds. flex: 1 1 0 governs the main axis, so in the grouped row it distributes width and leaves the height to --v — which is the whole mechanism. Turn the container to a column and the same declaration distributes height. Measured on the first build of this variant: Konstanz's three segments came out 106.7 px each, an exact third of the frame apiece, on values of 52, 24 and 12 %. A stacked chart drawn in equal thirds is not a faint fault — it is three numbers replaced by one — and it looks entirely like a chart. So a stacked block is flex: 0 0 auto and its height is its own again.
The shared contour costs one hairline per junction, and it is stated because it is arithmetic. Two segments that share their edge overlap by 1 px, so a three-part stack stands 2 px under the sum of its parts: measured at the 320 px frame, Konstanz's 88 % draws 278.8 px against a true 279.8. That is 0.35 % of the frame, which is under the weight of the line that causes it, and it is the same trade .cf-pie and .cf-blog-grid make for the same reason. A stack of more than three parts is a pie's question anyway.
The first legend item is the foot of the stack. A base belongs at the bottom and that is where a reader looks for it, so the series run bottom to top rather than top to bottom — which means the legend reads left to right as the stack reads upwards. It is stated here because it is the one place in this component where the legend's order and the drawing's order are not the same direction, and a figure that got it backwards would still look like a chart.
--read is for the grouped form. A number is printed above its own block, and above every segment but the last is the segment on top of it. The top segment could take one — except that the clamp above cannot help it: a segment's --v is its share, not the stack's total, so 100% / --block-hz is still one frame height but the segment's top is not --block-h of the way up, it is however far the segments under it reached. Nothing here can know that without the total being authored a second time, which is the one thing --v's own note forbids. A stack is read off its scale and its caption; every value stays in the accessibility tree, where it is read as what it is — a category, then its parts, in order.

What makes it readable

This is the one component in the system that uses hue to tell two data apart, so it carries the rules that make that legal. None of them is optional.

RuleWhy
Nothing is encoded in colour, at all Position identifies the series and the legend states the positions. The hue repeats what the order already says. Take every colour out of this figure — a forced palette does exactly that — and it is still readable, which is the test.
Every block carries its series name as text Position is not something a screen reader can be handed, so .cf-block__series puts the name in the tree on every block and is never painted. The tree reads “Konstanz · 2023 61 %, 2024 74 %, 2025 88 %” rather than three unattributed numbers. It is the only text in this system that exists only for the tree, and it is here because it is the label the drawing does not print — not a second copy of one that is.
The values are data, not decoration Cropped is not hidden. Every __val is real text in the tree at full strength; what --read changes is whether it is painted. A reader using a screen reader gets all fifteen numbers, and a reader looking at the picture gets a scale and the one number the caption is about.
Lime is emphasis, never a category The lit block is the one the caption names in words. Never light two, and never use lit / unlit to mean two kinds of thing — that is a second legend, carried by a colour, in the one figure that has already spent its colour budget.
The caption states the message, not the mechanics “Bremen hat in zwei Jahren 32 Punkte gutgemacht … und liegt damit erstmals über dem Mittel” — the claim, then what it rests on. A caption that only names the axes has left the reader to do the interpretation the figure was drawn to save them.

Contrast: the contours are --border-strong (black) and the frame and its subdivisions are --border-default, both on the page wash. Keys, legend items and steps are --text-secondary, 5.9:1 on CF-Grau — never --text-muted, which is 2.0:1 there and is the mistake Colour records. Printed values are --text-primary. The four faces are fills inside a black contour, so the 3:1 a UI shape owes is carried by the contour and not by the hue, which is the same arrangement the plot's lime cap has.

Growth

The blocks rise out of the floor as the figure arrives, scrubbed from scroll position rather than played on a timer — the same posture and the same fallbacks as .cf-plot, .cf-line and .cf-iso (→ Motion). One registered number, --block-grow, is animated on a view timeline, and every block's height reads it, so fifteen blocks grow as one thing rather than as fifteen animations that have to agree.

Only the blocks move. The frame, the scale, the legend, the keys and the bounds are drawn at once and stay drawn: they are the instrument, and the measurement is the only thing that arrives.

Every fallback is the finished chart. No support for view timelines, prefers-reduced-motion: reduce, and print all leave --block-grow at its registered 1. The growth also finishes inside the first third of the figure's cover range, so a block chart near the foot of a short page reaches full height rather than quietly understating every value in it.

Forced colours, and paper

This is the component where losing the palette is a design requirement rather than an accommodation, and it is worth being explicit about what happens. Forced colours discards every background image and does not repaint a background colour on a generic box, so all four ramps come off at once and every block in the figure is painted the same. A print path does the same unless the reader ticks “Background graphics”, which is off by default in every browser's print dialog, and this component does not ask for the ink back with print-color-adjust.

Nothing is lost, because nothing was carried by the hue. The series are still in their order inside every group, the legend still lists that order, the contours are borders and are forced to the reader's own ink, the scale still subdivides, and every value and every series name is still in the tree as text. What the reader loses is the colour, which is what the mode is for.

Measured in Chromium with both forced palettes emulated: every background-image resolved to none, every block's background-color to Canvas — white under the light palette and black under the dark one — every contour to CanvasText, and the frame and its steps to dashed. That last pair is the point. .cf-plot and .cf-pie both record having drawn themselves black on a black Canvas under the dark palette, because a custom property arriving at a paint is not forced; this figure's contours are borders, which are, and the one thing that is not — the occluding fill — is named Canvas by hand for exactly that reason.

The occlusion has to be named by hand. A background colour written as a custom property is not forced, so --surface-page would go on painting CF-Grau over a reader's chosen Canvas — a grey block on a black page, and the scale rules still running through it. Named Canvas, the block still hides what it stands in front of and does so in their palette. It is the same substitution .cf-plot makes for its bodies, for the same reason. The frame and the steps go dashed for .cf-line's reason: with one ink the frame's hairlines arrive at the contour's own weight, so instrument-against-datum is redrawn as the one distinction border-style can still express.

Do and don't

DoDon't
Use it when a reader has to compare two or three series across categories. Use it for one series of five values or fewer. That is the plot, it is drawn in space, and it is the better figure for a number somebody has to remember.
Keep the series ordinally related, and in the spectrum's order. Give three unrelated things three hues. A legend pretending to be a scale is worse than a table.
Stop at three series, and at five categories when there are three. Measured at a 320 px viewport — a 272 px frame, 240 of it past the gutter — that is an 8.7 px block, of which 6.7 is face inside its own contour. Six categories is 7.3, and that is where a block stops being one. Reach for a fourth hue. Past three the answer is four frames — .tiles with one .cf-block per cell, all on one stated scale — which is what the line does at its own ceiling.
State the ceiling and its unit in __bounds, always. Move the floor off zero to make a difference look bigger. A block's length is the quantity.
Let the scale carry the estimating and print one number. Set every block --read. Fifteen numerals over five categories is a table drawn badly.
Light the block the caption is about, if any. Light two, or light one per category. One lime moment per screen, and this figure is competing with everything else on the page for it.
Resize with --block-max. Resize with transform: scale(). It thins the contour, which is the one thing this component chose CSS to avoid.

Still open

  • The manual's own statistics plate is still not in the curated source slice. assets/source/manual/ holds 22 plates and none of them is the statistics and diagram plate the full archive is known to contain, which is the note the plot and the pie both carry. Everything here is derived from the parts of the language that are documented. Check it against that plate when the full archive is to hand. If the two disagree, the plate wins.
  • The plot's violett rake and these ramps are corrected by two different rules, and only one of them can be right. The plot draws Violett → Glas in SVG with the oklab waypoint at 19 % of the leg; the same leg in CSS owes the polar arc midpoint instead, because check-gradient-family.py classes it as a turn rather than a falloff. Both gates pass today — check_arc reads CSS only — so nothing is broken, but the system is applying two corrections to one leg depending on the medium it is drawn in. The block sidesteps it by not drawing that leg at all. It should be settled on Colour rather than per component.
  • Nothing holds the legend's order against the groups' order. The whole accessibility argument of this component rests on the series appearing in every group in the order the legend lists them, and that is exactly the invariant check-line-trace.py holds for the line's rungs. It is checkable — modifier sequence per __bars, against the sample sequence in __legend — and it is not checked yet. Until it is, a group authored in the wrong order renders as a perfectly ordinary chart that is wrong.
  • No negative values, and no second axis. A block hanging below the floor is drawable in this language and nothing yet needs one; the plot reached the same place and recorded it. A second value axis on the right is not drawable at all — it would need a second set of label rules running the other way, and two scales in one frame is the figure that lets an author make any two series agree.
