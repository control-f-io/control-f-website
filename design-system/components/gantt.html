Components

Gantt

A plan on one axis. The system could draw a value in space (Plot), a quantity over a domain (Line), parts of one whole (Pie) and one thing's way along a line (Progress); it had no way to put several of those on a shared span of time and say which one the work is standing in. Every mark here is one it already owns — the rail, the lattice cell, the field, and Annotation whole and unmodified — arranged against an axis.

The plan

Six phases against twenty-four weeks. The rows are level and unruled, the time grid under them is the field rather than a set of lines, and the only vertical drawn in ink is heute. Each phase carries its own dates as text, so the drawing is aria-hidden and adds nothing the row does not already say.

A tick names the line its column begins at, so six columns carry six labels and the far edge of the axis carries none. That edge is drawn — it is the field's horizon — and it is named in the caption, which is where the span of the whole figure belongs anyway.

How it is constructed

Nothing in the figure is a new mark. The run is the progress rail, the milestone is the lattice cell at rest, the ground is the field, and the layer that names a point on it is Annotation, whole. What the component adds is the axis they are all measured against.

The rows are level, and that is the plot's argument one axis over — the third figure in this section to reach it. Every direction in the isometric ground plane slopes on screen at ±26.57°, so a run marched along one of them trades drawn length for drawn height — and two phases of equal duration drawn at different depths come out different lengths. A chart whose measure depends on where it stands is not a chart. Time runs level here; the isometry stays in the marks standing on it. Line declines to draw a trace as a body for the same reason, and Pie stands its disc up rather than laying it on the ground plane.
The rows are not ruled, and that is the exact inverse of Table by the same test. A table rules its rows and draws nothing vertical, because there the row is the unit of reading and the rule between two of them is a division in the geometry chapter's sense: one thing ends, another begins, content on both sides. In a plan it is not. Every phase already has a drawn body of its own, and what is on both sides of a line between two rows is the same plan, on the same date, saying nothing. Drawn against the field's verticals it produces the one thing .cf-table names as the failure to avoid — a grid of boxes, which says spreadsheet. So this figure draws its verticals and rules nothing, the table rules its rows and draws no vertical, and both fall out of the same three-way test.
The time grid is not a line, it is the field. Where a line may go allows exactly three things — an edge, a division, a label rule — and calls everything else decoration. A column line is none of them: it closes no surface and separates no content. It passes for the reason the lattice passes on The Field: it is not a line in the layout at all, it is a texture on the ground, under everything, at a tenth of an ink. So it is drawn in --field-ink at --field-line — the same material .cf-ground is, inverting with the theme because that token does.

The 10 % ceiling comes with the material, and it is why the grid is --field-ink and not --border-subtle. tokens.css §8b derives that ceiling from the worst thing that has to cross the field — --text-secondary at 14 px on CF-Grau under a line: 4.71:1 at 10 %, 4.51:1 at 12 %, 4.15:1 at 16 %. --border-subtle is that 12 % row with no margin left in it, and it is a border token besides: reaching for it here would be claiming the grid is a line in the layout after arguing that it is not.

This figure then spends none of the cap. Every word in it is kept off the ground on purpose — keys to the left of the field, ticks above it, heute below the last row — so the only things crossing the grid are the runs themselves, which are ink rather than type. The ceiling is inherited whole and is not being leaned on.
heute is the one vertical drawn in ink, and it is a division in the strictest reading of that page. Done on one side of it, not done on the other, content on both. It also carries its own mono label, so it is a label rule as well — line and word, one mark. Everything the figure knows about when is on that line; everything it knows about where a plan stands is in the rungs. Two facts, two marks, and the distance between the head and the line is the only thing in the drawing that is neither.

The label hangs below the last row rather than above the axis. The scale's own labels are placed by the axis and this one is placed by the data, so above the rule the two collide at whatever date the plan happens to have reached — and a figure whose top edge is legible only in some months is not one.
The row pitch is the lattice cell's own height, and that is what puts an annotation on the lattice. --field-unit is 96 and the cell is 96 × 48, so a row of --space-12 is half a cell: a shallow (2, 1) leader out of a point on a run travels one whole cell across and half a row up, and every note in the figure comes down on the same lattice the runs are standing on. It is also why --annot-u on the notes layer is --gantt-row / 2 rather than the annotation component's own fluid unit.
This figure has an axis and Line has none, and the two are answering the same test with opposite hands. A trace is read at its points: every one is labelled where it stands, so a rule between two of them would be neither an edge, nor a division, nor a label rule — which is exactly why that component ends its own argument with no gridlines, no ticks, no axis. A plan is read the other way round. Its subject is extent, and a length has to be read against something or it is not a length. Hence a ground here, and hence that ground being the field rather than a set of rules — and hence the one line drawn in ink being the only one that is genuinely a division.

What the two figures share is the conclusion about colour. Line settles by measurement that a second series is one rung down the presence ladder and not one step down the grey ramp, because every grey light enough to read as a second series is under the 3:1 a contour owes. That is why four states of a plan are four line types and not four inks.
Two components draw today, and only one of them may be lit. Calendar's today is the found state's lit numeral at [aria-current="date"], and it claims the page's one lime moment — discharged by arithmetic, since a month has exactly one today. This component does not contest that: on a page carrying both, the calendar's day keeps the light and .cf-gantt__head is simply not written. Nothing is lost, because the head is emphasis and not information — the run's own solid-to-ghost boundary is drawn at --span-done either way and still says where the work has got to.

The drawings differ because the word means two things. In a month today is a slot — a numeral standing on its ground line, with the light picking it out of thirty others. In a plan today is a boundary, done on one side and not done on the other, which is a division, and a division is drawn as a rule.
What is deliberately not drawn is the dependency, and it was built first. A link between two adjacent rows has to drop exactly one row pitch, p. The brand sanctions two obliques and no others, so its horizontal run is either 2p — 96 px, the shallow step — or p/2, 24 px, the steep one. A finish-to-start dependency, which is most of them, has a horizontal run of zero. Every version of it therefore departs from inside the run above or arrives past the mark below; both are a line crossing a body that is not its own, and on a plan of six phases that is six of them.

Nothing is lost that the figure was carrying. The sequence is in the drawing already — where the runs stand, and the order of the rows — and it is in the text exactly, in the dates. What a plan actually wants at those points is a word, and that is the next note.
A leader in this system carries a word, so the step is spent on a note and not on a link. Annotation's own rule is that the line and the label are one mark. An unlabelled leader running between two runs is a leader with nothing to say — which is the decoration Where a line may go bans by name, in a figure that had just argued its way out of two other kinds of decoration. The same mark, given a word, is the thing the plan was missing.

Pie arrives at the same place from the other side, and the pair is worth reading together because the two figures fail the leader test for opposite reasons. A pie has no leaders because its anchors are measurements — a share's mid-angle lands on none of the four sanctioned slopes, so the line could not be drawn on the lattice even with a word on it, and proximity is made to do the linking. A plan's anchors are on the lattice by construction, so here the line can be drawn and the only question was whether it had anything to say. Both refuse the same thing: a leader not carrying a label.
The run draws out on a clip, never on a scaleX. Scaling a box scales the dash period inside it, so a 2-1 rung would arrive through every ratio except its own — the fault check-grow-origin.py describes for a dashed stroke under a scale, in the one medium where no vector-effect can take it back. The marks are the exception and they scale: a point has no length to draw along, and a cell coming up from nothing is the presence ladder's own figure.

The ladder, along one run

Four line types, one object drawn four times — which is what the Linien plate's bottom half is, and what tokens.css §8a names the presence ladder. The tokens say the ladder is only ever legible when the same object is drawn more than once, which is why no static page has needed it. A plan is six drawings of one object — a span of work — at different degrees of being real, on one sheet. It is the case the ladder was written for, and this is the first figure in the system to use all four rungs at once.

No axis and no heute below — one span of work, drawn four times, with nothing to measure it against. The two verticals are the field closing the run at both ends, which is what a plan of a single time column has.

RungLineWhat it says about the phase

The ladder is not the same axis as heute, and the figure would be misread if it were. A rung says how real the dates are. The now line says how much of the work is behind us. A finished phase and a fixed-date phase eight weeks out are the same drawing, on opposite sides of one line — which is correct, and is the reason the component has no --done modifier. An object may only move along the ladder, never across it.

Naming a point on it

A plan is a drawing, and the system already owns the layer that names a part of one. Annotation goes over a Gantt unchanged — same classes, same four quadrants, same leader — and .cf-gantt__notes is the only new thing: a layer that covers the plot area exactly and hands that component the plan's coordinates.

The annotation's own timeline had to be killed, and not for taste. .cf-annot-set animates --annot-draw on animation-timeline: view(), which resolves against the nearest scrollport — and inside this component that is .cf-gantt__scroll. overflow-x: auto leaves overflow-y computing to auto, so the box is a scroll container in both axes and its block axis never scrolls. That is exactly the .cf-hero defect check-overflow-clip.py was written for: the timeline resolves, it reports a progress, and the progress does not move. So the animation is cancelled — which is the only way to get the property back, since a custom property cannot override an animation — and --annot-draw is derived from --gantt-draw. 3x − 2 puts the labels in the last third of the plan's own draw, which keeps the order the annotation states in prose: the drawing is finished before it is labelled.
The layer is deliberately not named cf-annot-fig. That container name switches the annotation to its legend fallback below 28rem — the right answer for a 320 px drawing, and the wrong one here, because the plot area is under 28rem exactly when the plan is scrolling. A static legend inside an absolutely positioned layer is not a legend. A plan answers a narrow screen by scrolling; it does not fold.
Three marks in this figure are the same rhombus, and that is the reading rather than a collision. The lattice cell means a point, here as everywhere else in the system. What is attached to it says which kind: nothing at all is a milestone, light is where the work has got to, and a leader with a word on the end of it is a note. One glyph, three attachments — which is why the anchor takes --gantt-node and is drawn at the same size as the other two rather than shrunk to distinguish itself from them.
Two marks are closest at the figure's floor, not at the width you drew it at. The axis compresses to --gantt-cols × --gantt-col-min and then the scroll box takes over, so a note anchored four days from the head has its least room there and nowhere else. A rhombus is about 17 px across whatever the axis is doing, which makes the floor the only width worth checking two marks in the same row against — and the reason the note below is anchored at the end of the run rather than in the middle of the ghost it names.
Point into empty ground, and stay inside the frame. The author picks the quadrant the same way they do on a drawing — most of a plan is empty, and the empty part is usually the row above or below the run being named. Two things are stricter here than on an illustration: a note that overflows the figure is inside a scroll box and will be cut or will add a scrollbar, and the lit rung is already spent — the head is the plan's one lime moment, so notes on a plan with a live phase are contour. On a plan with none, one note may take .cf-annot--lit.

What makes it readable

A plan is a chart, and a chart is the one component where the picture can be perfectly rendered and the component still fail. The plot states four rules for that; three of them are the same here and the fourth is about the axis.

RuleWhy
The text is the data, and there is no second copy of it Each <li> carries its phase and its dates as real text, so the figure reaches the accessibility tree as an ordered list of "Aufnahme 02.02. – 20.02.", "Datenmodell 23.02. – 27.03. · läuft", "Freigabe Datenmodell 27.03. · Meilenstein", … The drawing says nothing the row does not, which is why the track and the scale are aria-hidden. No visually hidden table, nothing that can drift out of step with the picture.
Every rung is stated in words as well as in a line geplant, Vorschlag, läuft, and the milestone that says Meilenstein. A dash ratio is a difference some readers cannot resolve and every reader has to be taught; the word costs four characters in a column that is already there.
Lime is emphasis, never a category The head is the one lit mark and the caption is about it. Never light two, and never use lit / unlit to mean two kinds of phase — that is a legend, in a figure that has none, carried by a colour a share of readers cannot separate from its neighbours. On a page that has already spent its lime elsewhere — a Calendar's today, most of all — leave the head out and let the run's own solid-to-ghost boundary carry the same fact.
The scroll box can be reached without a mouse An axis is data, so a plan legitimately outgrows its column. The box carries tabindex="0" and takes its name from the caption — without the tabindex, everyone who does not drag with a pointer never sees the far end of the plan. WCAG 2.1.1, the same construction Table uses.
The caption states the message, not the mechanics "Das Datenmodell liegt vier Tage hinter dem Plan — die Freigabe Ende März hält, weil der Rückstand kleiner ist als der Puffer davor." The claim, then what it rests on. A caption that only names the date range has left the reader to do the interpretation the figure was drawn to save them.

Contrast: the runs, the marks and the now line are all --border-strong on the page wash. Keys are --text-primary; dates and ticks are --text-secondary, 5.9:1 on CF-Grau — never --text-muted, which is 2.0:1 there. No type in the figure stands on the grid at all, which is deliberate: the field's 10 % ceiling is inherited and left unspent. The lit head is a ramp inside a black contour, so the 3:1 a UI shape owes is carried by the contour and not by the lime.

Drawing

The plan draws itself out of its own start as the figure arrives, scrubbed from scroll position rather than played on a timer — the same posture as .cf-plot and .cf-iso (→ Motion). One registered number, --gantt-draw, is animated on a view timeline; the run's clip, the marks' scale and the annotation layer's own --annot-draw all read it, so they cannot fall out of step.

The ground does not arrive. The field, the axis and the now line are drawn at full strength from the first frame, because they are not the data — they are what the data is measured against, and a figure whose axis fades in is a figure that measures nothing for the first half of its own animation.

Every fallback is the finished plan. No support for view timelines, prefers-reduced-motion: reduce, and print all leave --gantt-draw at its registered 1. The growth also finishes inside the first third of the figure's cover range, so a plan near the foot of a short page reaches its full length rather than stopping short and understating every phase in it.

Do and don't

DoDon't
Use it for a plan a reader should be able to hold in their head — five to nine phases. Reach for it for a project schedule. Past about nine rows the figure stops being a picture of a plan and becomes a tool for editing one, which is a different product.
Give every phase its dates in the key column. Leave the drawing to carry the dates and make the reader measure them off the axis. There is no axis in this system that exists to be estimated against.
Draw a run in contour, at 2 px, on the ladder. Fill it. A filled bar is the generic Gantt, and contour before fill is the first rule this brand has.
Mark one phase --live, and let its head sit where the work has actually got to. Mark two, or pin the head to the now line. A head that is always on the line is a mark with nothing to say — the gap between the two is the reading.
Divide the axis into equal columns and label them. Label six columns as six calendar months. They are 28 to 31 days long, the columns are not, and a two-day lie in a figure whose whole content is duration is the same defect as a plot whose columns do not track their values. Weeks, or four-week steps, are equal by construction.
Say the one thing the drawing cannot with a note — the float, the ghost, the reason a date moved. Join two runs with a line. It is a leader with no word on it, and there is no angle in this brand that gets from one run's end to the next one's start without crossing a body.
Keep keys to one line. Let a key wrap. The row pitch is the unit every annotation's step is measured in, so a taller row puts its notes off the lattice the runs stand on.

Still open

  • The manual has no plan plate either. The 22 plates in assets/source/manual/ hold no chart of any kind — which is already noted on Plot and on Table, and is as true here. Everything on this page is derived from the parts of the language that are documented: the lattice, the four angles, the four line types, the light layer, the field. Check it against the statistics plate when the full archive is to hand. If the two disagree, the plate wins.
  • Dependencies are not drawn, and the reason is in How it is constructed rather than here. What is genuinely open is whether they can be drawn at all in this language. Every path that keeps both sanctioned obliques crosses a body; a path that does not would need a third angle, and the brand has four and no more. Until there is one, the sequence is carried by position and by the dates, and the leader is spent on a word.
  • Nothing holds a note to the row it is written for. --annot-y is a length, and calc(var(--gantt-row) * 2.5) is the third row's centre only as long as the author counts correctly — a note half a row out looks exactly like a note pointing between two phases, which is a thing somebody might mean. It is countable in the markup: every --annot-y in a .cf-gantt__notes should be an odd multiple of half the row pitch. That is the shape every check in scripts/ has, and this one is not written.
  • No overlap, no swimlanes, one run per row. A phase that stops and restarts is two rows here. Two runs in one row is drawable — the marks are already positioned independently — but the head reads --span-from and --span-to off the row, so a second run would need a second source of truth, which is the one thing this component is built to avoid.
  • The float between two phases is not drawn. Slack is the empty axis between one run's end and the next one's start, and it is currently just air with a note over it where it matters. The ghost rung would draw it in one declaration; nothing yet needs it, and a figure that draws everything it could draw is the thing this system is trying not to be.
