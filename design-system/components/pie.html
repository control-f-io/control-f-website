Components

Pie

Parts of one whole. The plot answers how do these compare; nothing in the system answered what is this made of. A share is not a value on a scale — it is a piece of something, and the thing a reader has to be able to see is the whole it is a piece of. So the figure is a ring, and the hole in it is where the whole is finally stated.

The ring

Three to five shares of one total. The drawing is the data: no legend, no axis, no key. Every share is labelled against its own arc with its own number, the total stands in the middle with its unit, and the ring has a body for the light to land on. One share is lit — the one the caption is about — and the light moves to whichever share the reader points at.

The three codings

One ramp, cut at two places, and the third coding is the absence of the first. Nothing here is a colour per share: every unlit share is painted identically, so there is nothing a legend could be made of and nothing encoded in a distinction a third of readers cannot draw. What the codings separate is kinds of surface — material, lit material, and no material — which is the same three-way the rest of the system draws objects with.

CodingWhere it comes fromWhat it says
#cf-pie-ground
every share		 CF-Grau → Glas. The bloom's own ramp without its lime leg, renormalised over the band — the construction --gradient-foil already makes for .text-foil, which drops Weiss and rebases off the hot spot. This is a surface. The ring has a body, and the light has something to land on.
#cf-pie-light
--lit, and whatever the pointer is on		 CF-Grau → Glas → lime. The whole ramp at the mid rake, with the one oklab waypoint the lime leg owes. This is the share the caption is about. One per figure, per moment.
.cf-pie--hollow No paint on the body. The cuts, the contours and the numbers are untouched. The ground is already carrying material and one more surface would be one too many.
The ground is the ramp minus its lime leg, and it needs no waypoint. Every leg leaving lime in an SVG carries one extra stop at 19 % of the leg, because <linearGradient> has no in oklab and lime → Glas is ΔEok 0.045 between the two paths. Drop the lime and the surviving leg is Glas → CF-Grau, which is ΔEok 0.00073 — the two colour spaces agree on it to three decimal places, and check-gradient-family.py asks nothing of a leg that leaves no lime. Colour lists it as "the tail of every rake"; the ground coding is that tail, and nothing else.

The same component with .cf-pie--hollow on the <figure>. Every body loses its paint and nothing else changes: the cuts, the contours, the numbers, the lit share and the pointer all behave exactly as above, and the arcs are still the hit targets they were when they were painted.

The light follows the pointer

Point at a share — at its arc, or at the word naming it — and the light moves there. Take the pointer away and it goes home. The authored share gives its light up for exactly as long as the pointer is on a different one, so no reading of this figure ever holds two lime moments, and the rule that says so is arithmetic rather than a note somebody has to keep: --pie-arc is a max() of the two sources, and the one the pointer is not on is travelling to zero.

It reveals nothing, and that is the point rather than a shortfall. Every key and every number is printed at every moment; what the pointer moves is emphasis, not information. That is Mobile's nothing is hover-only in its strongest form — remove hover entirely and the figure is exactly the figure this component shipped without it — and it is why five spans that go nowhere do not each get a tabindex. :focus-within is named beside :hover because every state pair in components.css names both, and here it means a share a page has wrapped in a link lights from the keyboard the way it lights from the pointer.

What it is good for is the pie's one genuine ambiguity: which arc belongs to which label, at the small end of the figure. A 9 % share is 32° of ring and its name stands beside it — pointing at either lights the other.
The hit target is geometry the figure already had. Each body is dashed to exactly its own share, and all three engines hit-test a dashed stroke on the drawn dash and let the gap fall through to whatever lies under it — measured on Chromium 151, Firefox 154 and WebKit 26.5, at four points on one ring. So pointing at an arc points at the share that arc owns, with no second shape to keep in step with the first and nothing per-value to author.

The share is not the hit target, and it took one declaration to stop being one. In the tier that rides the ring each <li> is inset: 0 — it covers the whole drawing — so left alone the last share in the markup takes every pointer event in the figure and the other four are unreachable at every angle. .cf-pie__seg is therefore pointer-events: none, and the band and the label turn it back on. The ink and the frame are none for the same reason from the other side: both cover the drawing and both sit above the bands, so an <svg> box that hit-tested would seal every share underneath it. :hover still reaches the <li> from a descendant that is a target — in all three engines, pointer-events decides what is hit, not which ancestors the hover chain runs through.

How it is constructed

Every measure in the ring is a measure the plot already carries. The ring is 6u across — the same 6u a column at --v:1 stands, so a pie and a plot in one article are drawn at one scale. The band is 1u deep, the unit cube's vertical edge. The hole is 4u across, the plot's column pitch. The cuts are the system's 1 px contour, the numbers are the treatment .cf-plot__val already gives every figure, and the light is the bloom doing exactly the job Colour gives it.

The figure is face-on, and that is an isometric decision rather than a retreat from one. Laying the disc on the 2:1 ground plane is the obvious move in this brand, and it is the one thing a chart of angles cannot survive. The projection is scaleY(0.5), so a ray at t from the horizontal is drawn at atan(0.5 · tan t). Area ratios survive an affine squash — but the angle a reader actually judges a pie by does not. Two true 30° shares, the same 8.3 % of the whole:
ShareTrueDrawn on the ground planeReads as
0° → 30°30.00°16.10°4.5 % of a turn
60° → 90°30.00°49.11°13.6 % of a turn
A factor of 3.05 between two equal numbers, decided by nothing but where on the ring they happened to land. Extrude that disc into a cylinder and the near shares gain a visible side wall the far ones do not have — the same lie again, with a second mechanism behind it. So the pie stands up: a true circle, --radius-full, the single curve Geometry allows. It is the finding the plot already made about the level row, one dimension along.
No leaders, and that is the "where a line may go" rule doing its job. .cf-annot draws a leader from an anchor to a note, and it is always on the lattice — 26.57° or 63.43°, one of the four angles. A share's mid-angle is a measurement, so a leader off it would land on none of the four, and a line that is neither an edge nor a division nor a label rule is decoration. Each label therefore stands against its own arc and nothing else: proximity is the link, which is what direct labelling means. It is also why a key is one word, the same discipline the plot's five keys keep.

The placement is one declaration, and the second half of it is the part that matters: the label box is pushed out of its own centre by half its own size along the same radius, so its near edge lands on the anchor whichever way the anchor points. No per-quadrant class, no branch, nothing to author. sin() and cos() are what make that expressible — the same capability, and the same @supports posture, the annotation already takes for the isometric angle.
Without CSS trig the labels become a legend, and that is authored rather than inherited. A share's mid-angle is a measurement, so there is no literal to fall back on the way .cf-annot falls back on 26.565°. The base tier therefore ships the labels as a ruled list under the drawing, in ring order: a worse figure than the one this component exists to build, and a readable one.

The alternative was to author the ring placement in the base tier and let the gated tier refine it, which is exactly the mistake acts.css shipped and check-fallback-tier.py now holds — a declaration authored in the base tier is a declaration the base tier ships. Authored there, position: absolute with a transform nobody can compute puts four labels and the total on one point. Two consequences follow, and both are one declaration: the ring takes its height from a ::before rather than from a height, so the box is 6u of drawing in one tier and 6u plus the list in the other; and the total in the hole is centred on 3u rather than on 50 %, because 50 % of the taller box is halfway down the list.
The sweep is not a dash, and the reason is measurable. pathLength="1" with stroke-dasharray: 1 is this system's draw mechanism — it is what .cf-iso__trace uses, and check-line-types.py allows the literal 1 by value so that a second drawing may use it. It is measured in user units. But vector-effect: non-scaling-stroke, which is what keeps a 1 px hairline 1 px at every size, measures a dash in screen pixels. Put the two on one path and the drawn fraction is out by the viewBox scale: measured here at --pie-u: 32px, a full-circle dash on a non-scaling contour drew 0.625 of the ring — 120/192, the scale factor exactly.

So the two never meet. The contours and the cuts are hairlines and keep non-scaling-stroke; the band is 1u deep, scales with the drawing, and is the one path here that may be dashed. .cf-iso__trace is exempted from non-scaling-stroke by name for this exact reason, and the exemption was written down long before this component needed it.
The dash offset is multiplied into a length, and Gecko is why. stroke-dashoffset takes a <length-percentage> or a <number>, and Firefox 153 accepts the number only as a literal: every unitless calc() on this property is invalid at computed-value time and drops to the initial 0 — including calc(1 - 0.27), with no custom property in it at all. On a dash of 1 over pathLength="1", an offset of 0 is the whole ring lit: not a share drawn short, a share drawn as every share there is. Measured on the same markup:

The light is the bloom, not the rake, and that is what survives being turned. The band is rotated to wherever its share begins, so a linear rake painted across it would swing its axis by whatever the data happened to say — off all four brand angles, at an angle nobody chose. A bloom centred on the ring's own centre looks identical at every --turn. It is also the member of the family Colour hands to "a body of revolution", which face-on is exactly what this is: mirrored, lime on the rim and CF-Grau toward the centre, at the mid rake.

The band spans r 40 → 60 of a 60-unit radius, so the ramp's own 0 → 49 → 100 % lands at 0.667 → 0.83 → 1, and the oklab waypoint at 19 % of the lime leg lands at 0.9677. Those four numbers are derived from the ramp rather than picked, and check-gradient-family.py recomputes them. The ground coding is the same bloom with its lime leg dropped — see The three codings.
The figure paints in three layers, and the layers are kinds of mark rather than shares. Every band runs from its own cut to the next share's, so a band always ends on a hairline belonging to somebody else — and a 20-user-unit stroke laid over a 1 px contour wins by 30 to 1. Which band wins which seam is then decided by which <li> came later, and the wrap-round seam at 12 o'clock is decided the wrong way by construction: the last share's band is above the first share's cut, always, in every figure.

The version this replaces answered that with z-index: 0 on the lit share, which held while exactly one share could be filled and that share was authored. Neither is true now — every share has a body, and the light goes to whichever share the reader points at — and a per-share number cannot say "under all of the ink" when the share is chosen at pointer time. So the ring is a stacking context of its own and the marks sort inside it by kind:
LayerWhat is on it
0 — the fills.cf-pie__draw: every share's body, every share's lit face
1 — the ink.cf-pie__ink: every cut. .cf-pie__frame: both contours
2 — the type.cf-pie__label, .cf-pie__whole
The <li> deliberately carries no z-index: give it one and it becomes a stacking context, the three marks are sealed inside it, and the figure sorts by share again. Contour before fill is then a fact about the figure rather than about markup order, which is what the rule always meant.
The lit share does not glow, and the measurement is why. The spill is a small mark's light: --glow-r is 6 px on an icon, 8 px on a relay head, 12 px on a plot cap — and .cf-iso__light, the lit face of every object in the system, has never carried it at all. This band is the largest lit surface the system has. Rendered at --pie-u: 52px with the plot cap's 12 px, the bloom landed on both sides of the outer contour and the 1 px hairline over the lit share went from black-on-lime to invisible — on the one arc where a reader most needs to see where the share ends. The brand's first rule is the contour, so the light gives way to it. It is a lit face, and it is treated like every other lit face here.
Two numbers per share, and a script that checks the arithmetic. --v is the share; --turn is where it starts, which is the running sum of every --v before it. CSS cannot take that sum — it cannot see a preceding sibling's value — so the author writes it, and an author writing a running sum by hand is an author who will one day write 0.72 for 0.73. Nothing about that failure is visible: a ring whose cuts are 1 % out of step still looks like a ring. scripts/check-pie-shares.py is the thing that looks — it re-adds every figure in the tree and fails on a --turn that is not the running sum, on shares that do not total 1, on more than five, on more than one lit, and on a share below the 5 % floor.
The floor is the pie's own, and it is higher than the plot's. A plot has to fit five columns and four gaps — 18u — so its unit floors at 16 px. A pie is 6u of ring plus a label hanging off each side, however many shares it has: 6u + 2 × (0.5u + --pie-label-w). At u = 24 that is 328 px, against the 335 px a 375 px viewport leaves inside its gutters. The cap and the crossover are the plot's, so the two figures still grow together and finish at the same size.
The sum above is a 16 px row, and the label carries its own second ceiling. Two of its three terms are px and the third is rem, so the figure the floor sizes is 328 px at a 16 px browser default, 368 px at 20 and 408 px at 24 — while the ring it is measured against floors at 24 px and does not move. A ceiling that grows with the type it caps never binds: on the shipped landing page at 320 px the widest key measured 74 px against 80 at a 16 px default, 92 against 100 at 20, and 111 against 120 at 24, and the document scrolled sideways by 6 px and 23 px on the last two while WARTUNG / €0,50 Mio. lost 13 px and then 31 px off the left edge, where a negative offset costs no scroll width and no overflow gate can see it. So the tier that rides the ring takes the smaller of two ceilings: --pie-label-w, and 50vw − --pie-label-r × |sin(a)| — the room the label's own angle leaves it between a centred figure's centre and the screen edge, which is everything at 3 and 9 o'clock and nothing at 12 and 6. The angle is why it is not simply a tighter --pie-label-w: a flat px cap narrow enough to fit 320 px at a 24 px default is 76 px, and 76 px wraps the 6 o'clock label — where the room was never short — into a third line and out through the ring's bottom margin. Measured after, 320 px, the same four defaults: 0, 0, 0 and 57 px, the last of them the landing act's own rem insets rather than this figure's.

What makes it readable

A chart is the one component where the picture can be perfectly rendered and the component still fail. The plot's four rules hold here unchanged, and the pie adds a fifth that is its own.

RuleWhy
The whole is stated This is the ring's entire justification over a filled disc. A pie tells a reader what the shares are of each other and has never once told them what the whole is — 46 % of what, and is that a lot? Four units of cleared ground in the middle of the figure is where that sentence goes, and it goes there with its unit. A pie without it is a figure that cannot be checked.
The text is the data, and there is no second copy of it Each <li> carries its key and its value as real text, so the figure reaches the accessibility tree as the total followed by an ordered list of "Netz 46 %", "Erzeugung 27 %", … The drawing adds nothing the text does not already say, which is why it is aria-hidden. No visually hidden table, no duplicated markup that can drift out of step with the picture.
Every share is labelled where it stands Direct labelling rather than a legend, which is what lets a reader take a number off the figure at all — and a legend is the specific failure a pie is famous for, because matching six swatches to six arcs is the work the figure was drawn to save. It is also why the pie needs no key.
Lime is emphasis, never a category The lit share is the one the caption is about. Because the caption names it in words, nothing is encoded in colour alone. Never colour the shares against each other. One lit and the rest in one ground is emphasis; six colours round a ring is a legend, in a component that has no legend, carried by hues a third of readers cannot separate — and Sky and Violett are not UI colours in this system in any case. Contour and the printed number carry the whole comparison.

The ground coding does not touch this, and the test is that it is the same paint on every unlit share: there is nothing to look up, no second key, and no share distinguished from another by anything but its arc and its number. What it adds is a surface, not a category. → The three codings
The caption states the message, not the mechanics "Verteilung der 2025 ausgewerteten Messreihen nach Domäne. Fast die Hälfte kommt aus dem Netz…" — the claim, then what it rests on. A caption that only names the total has left the reader to do the interpretation the figure was drawn to save them.

Contrast: the contours and the cuts are --border-strong (black) on the page wash. Values are --text-primary; keys are --text-secondary, 5.9:1 on CF-Grau — never --text-muted, which is 2.0:1 there and is the mistake documented on Colour. Both bands are fills inside a black contour, so the 3:1 a UI shape owes is carried by the contour and not by the lime or the Glas. No text ever stands on a band: the keys and values are outside the rim and the whole is inside the hole, which is what keeps the fills out of every contrast calculation in this figure.

Forced colours takes the fills and keeps the drawing, and fixing that turned up a defect older than the fills. A custom property arriving at an SVG stroke is not forced. Measured in Chromium 151, forced colours active, prefers-color-scheme: dark, this page over http: .cf-pie__contour computed rgb(0, 0, 0) against a Canvas of rgb(0, 0, 0) — both contours and every cut, absent rather than faint, in the palette where a drawing has nothing else to be read by. The same finding the found state's five rungs carried, in a component that draws instead of marks, and it takes the same answer: CanvasText is what ink means in this palette.

A paint server is not forced either — stroke computes url(#cf-pie-ground) in both palettes — so the bands would go on painting their own Glas and lime under a reader's chosen black, which is where the ink would become unreadable again the moment it was fixed. So the fills come off and what is left is exactly .cf-pie--hollow. Counted as distinct colours in the ring's own crop, the two palettes are now each other's mirror: 2 761 ink pixels in light, 2 761 in dark, where dark had none of the drawing at all before.

The light arrives, and then it moves

The ring, both contours, every cut, every body and every label are at their true values in the first frame and never move. The one thing that travels is the light, and it travels twice: it arrives on the authored share as the reader comes to the figure, and it moves to whatever share the reader points at. Both are the same fill along the same arc, and both obey the rule .cf-iso__light already states in as many words: lime is light — it is the last thing to arrive, and it arrives as a fill coming up, not as an object moving.

Driven byRateReads
the arrival scroll position, cover 30 % → 48 % the reader's own scroll The same window .cf-iso__light takes, so the light lands when the figure is already up rather than on the way in.
the move :hover / :focus-within --duration-slow, 480 ms The token's own definition is "a reveal, or light crossing a surface", and light crossing a surface is the entire content of this transition.

Scrubbed rather than played on a timer, the same posture as .cf-iso and .cf-plot (→ Motion). The two properties that carry the light are registered <number>, so what interpolates is a number and what redraws is one stroke-dashoffset per frame. Nothing reflows, and nothing but the light moves.

Two sources, one light, and a max() instead of a rule. When the pointer moves from one share to another, the first share's light drains back to its own start cut while the second fills from its own — two fills, in opposite directions, on one ring. The :not() in the second selector is what stops the lit share dipping when you point at it: without it --pie-home would fall while --pie-point rose, and max() would bottom out at 0.5 halfway through — a share that darkens when you point at it.
The ring used to divide as it arrived, and that was a wrong picture rather than an intermediate one. The first build turned every cut out of 12 o'clock into its own share, which read well and is the figure's own idea — a whole, being divided. A label rides its own share, so at --pie-grow: 0 all four stacked on top of each other at 12 o'clock and only untangled around half the range. Holding the labels still while the cuts travel is worse again: each label then names the arc beside it, and for half a second the arc beside it belongs to somebody else. A chart may be incomplete while it arrives. It may not be wrong while it arrives.
Every fallback is the finished figure, and so is the floor. No support for view timelines, prefers-reduced-motion: reduce, and print all leave --pie-grow at its registered 1. Note what the other end is: at --pie-grow: 0 the figure is still the finished ring, every body at its real value, with only the light missing. There is no frame of this animation in which the chart says something untrue.

The move opts into motion the way .cf-subdiv does — the transition lives inside @media screen and (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference), so a reader who has asked for less still gets the light where the pointer is, without the 480 ms of it getting there. On paper there is no pointer, and the figure is the authored one.

Do and don't

DoDon't
Use it when the message is what this is made of — three to five parts of one stated total. Use it to compare. Two arcs at different angles on a ring is the hardest comparison in graphics; that is what the plot is for, and a pie whose caption says "more than" should have been one.
Make the shares add to the whole. Draw a ring of things that are not parts of one total — two years, two scenarios, anything that could exceed 100 %. The hole then states a number that is not true.
Keep the smallest share at 5 % or above, and fold the tail into one Sonstige. Draw a 2 % sliver. Its label has nowhere to stand — two stacked lines are about 5 % of the label circle — and its arc is shorter than the word naming it.
Light the share the caption is about, if any. Light two. One lime moment per object, and this figure is competing with everything else on the page for it.
Order the shares as the data orders them, or largest first from 12 o'clock. Order them so the lit one lands somewhere flattering. The ring runs clockwise from 12 o'clock and that start is --angle-square, not a choice per figure.
Resize with --pie-u. Resize with width or transform: scale(). Both thin the contour, which is the brand's first rule.
Reach for --hollow when the ground under the figure is already carrying material — a dark section, artwork, a drawing beside it. Reach for it to make the figure quieter in general. The body is the surface the light lands on, and a ring with no body spends its whole contrast budget on one lit arc.
Let the pointer be the thing that answers "which arc is this?". Put anything only the pointer can reach into the figure. Every key and every number is printed at every moment, and hover is emphasis moving — the day it carries a fact is the day the figure stops working on a phone.
Give a second pie on the same page its own gradient ids. Copy the <defs> verbatim. Two elements may not share one id, and a duplicate resolves to whichever came first — which renders correctly, right up until the two figures want different paint.

Still open

  • The manual's own statistics plate is not in the curated source slice. assets/source/manual/ holds 22 plates and none of them is the statistics and diagram plate the full archive is known to contain — the same gap the plot records. Everything here is therefore derived from the parts of the language that are documented: the unit, the 1 px contour, the four angles, the bloom, the one curve. Check it against that plate when the full archive is to hand. If the two disagree, the plate wins.
  • --turn is a running sum an author has to take. CSS cannot see a preceding sibling's value, so the second number is written rather than derived. check-pie-shares.py makes the mistake loud, which is the answer available today, not the answer. sibling-index() would not help; what this needs is a running total, and nothing proposed offers one.
  • One series only, and no comparison of two wholes. Two concentric rings is the obvious extension and the one most likely to reintroduce exactly the misreading this component was built to avoid: an outer arc is longer than an inner arc at the same share, so the ring nearer the rim wins every comparison by construction. Not attempted.
  • No share below 5 %, so no long tail. The floor is a label-clearance number, not a statistical one. A distribution that is genuinely a long tail is a table, or a plot of the top five.
