The Sheet

The brand's founding sentence is that this sits on a data space and not on a sheet of paper. Printed, it becomes the one thing it said it was not, and the system had never decided what it is there. It had decided it twenty times instead. This chapter is the sentence those twenty decisions were each half of: paper carries two of the six layers — contour and text — and nothing drawn as one object may be divided by a sheet boundary.

Where this comes from

No plate in assets/source/manual/ is about print, and neither mockup is a sheet, so this chapter is derived from the language the way The Found State and Plot are: built only out of what the manual does establish. Its one source is the Materials plate — the six layers, back to front — and its one move is to ask which of them a sheet can hold.

What makes it worth deriving is that the answer was already being given, one object at a time, by people who could not see each other's work. The tree this chapter was written against carried twenty @media print blocks across three stylesheets. The foil goes black. The solid button gives up its plate. The stream's caret stops and its screen-reader copy comes out of the clip rect. The consent banner goes, and so does the height it had reserved. Every one of those is right, and every one of them is about an object. Not one of them is about the sheet: in 28 558 lines there was no @page, no break-inside, no break-after, no orphans and no widows.

Printing is not a rare path for this site. The pages a reader actually puts on paper are a job advert they want to read away from a screen, an Impressum or a Datenschutz page they have been asked to keep, and an article they want to mark up. Three of those four are legal text.

The law: paper carries two of the six layers

Materials stacks the system back to front as base wash, opaque, frosted glass, contour, light, text. A sheet holds the last two of those and nothing else.

01 Base washCF-Grau to white, plus grainWithdrawn A wash is a ground, and paper already has one.
02 OpaqueBlack and anthracite platesWithdrawn A black plate is the reader's ink, and the brand does not get to spend it.
03 Frosted glassBlur over what is behindWithdrawn Glass is a thing you see through. There is nothing behind a sheet.
04 Contour1 px hairlines, four line typesCarried The one layer that was already ink. It is what print is best at.
05 LightLime, once per screenWithdrawn Lime on white is 1.1:1, and the light layer was never a surface.
06 TextGeist, Geist MonoCarried —

Two survivors, and they are the two the brand's first rule is about. Kontur vor Fläche is not a compromise on paper — it is the only thing paper was ever going to let this system be. A page of this site printed correctly is the brand with everything optional taken off it.

Ink is data, or it is nothing

The second clause follows from the first, and the system was already living it. Every browser's print dialog ships Background graphics off, so a background is a request rather than a fact — and no rule in this system asks for it back. print-color-adjust: exact appears nowhere, deliberately. Block states the reason in its own words:

a chart that demands ink to be readable is a chart that is not readable

So a colour never carries the only copy of anything. Where a screen distinguishes with hue, a sheet distinguishes with a line — the same trade every forced-colors rule in this system already makes, paid to a different reader. A figure that survives forced colours survives paper, and the two checks are one check.

The one thing this clause is not is permission to leave an object unpainted. The primary button's only paint was a gradient over a transparent border, so with backgrounds off the site's single call to action printed as a phrase floating on white with nothing around it. It takes a black contour on paper now: what a colour was carrying, a line carries.

The page box

There had been no page margin of the system's own — only the browser's default, and then --gutter on top of it, a clamp that reaches 5rem and was measured against a viewport rather than a sheet. Stacked against Chromium's default that is about 33 mm a side. Two boxes were holding one margin. 

@page {
  margin: 16mm 14mm;
}
@media print {
  .container { padding-inline: 0; }
  body { background: none; }
  .page-wash { background-image: none; }
}

Millimetres, because the two papers this will meet disagree about inches: A4 is 210 × 297 mm and US Letter is 216 × 279. A margin written in mm is the same margin on both; a margin written in in is not. 14 mm at the sides leaves 182 mm of measure on A4 and 188 on Letter, and 16 mm head and foot is the band a hand holds a sheet by without covering a line. No size is declared — the paper in the tray is the reader's decision, not the brand's.

The ground is two layers and neither had been withdrawn. body carries --surface-base, which is CF-Grau: the data space itself, as a flat fill behind every sheet. .page-wash carries the ramp and --grain over it, and the reason that one has to be said out loud is measured in Colour: --grain is a data-URI SVG, and a url() layer beside a gradient carries the gradient through filters that drop a gradient on its own. The reader who ticks Background graphics — the single tick that exists, and the one you tick when you want a figure to keep its colour — was buying the full CF-Grau ramp and 8 % greyscale noise flooded across every sheet along with it.

What a sheet does not carry

Everything in this register is an affordance rather than content — a thing whose whole purpose is to be operated. On a sheet it cannot be operated, so it is not faint, it is absent. That is the standard the hero's pause switch is already held to: a switch whose state cannot change what the reader sees is worse than no switch at all.

And the three things the hero did for the nav

These have to be given back on the same line the nav is taken away, because none of them was ever a decision about paper — they are the nav's decisions, and the nav is not here. The hero is pulled up by exactly --nav-height so the artwork starts where the page starts, and it pads the same distance back so its first line is not under the plate. With the plate gone, the pull crops 84 px of artwork off the top of the sheet and the pad returns it as white.

The third is the largest single change on the sheet. min-height: min(92vh, 56rem, …) resolves against the page box in a print context, so at A4 under the margins above it is min(~922px, 896px) — an 896 px reservation for about 400 px of content. That is most of sheet 1 of every printed page in this site spent on one headline. A hero is a first screen; a sheet is not a screen.

What may not be cut

This is the clause nothing had made, and it is the one that decides whether a printout is usable. A component cannot know it is being cut. Fragmentation is a property of the sheet and not of the object standing on it: a job card is not divided because of anything the card says, it is divided because it began 40 mm from the bottom of a page. There is no rule you could write next to .cf-vacancy that would be about .cf-vacancy.

Read the register as the answer to a single question asked of each component: if a sheet boundary landed in the middle of this, would the reader be looking at one thing in two places, or at two things?

RuleApplies toWhy
break-after: avoid h1, h2, h3, h4, h5, h6, figcaption,
.cf-section-header, .cf-page-header		 A heading is not a line, it is the top of the thing under it. Left alone, the last thing on a sheet is regularly a section number and its title with the section itself overleaf.
orphans: 3; widows: 3 p, li, blockquote, dd Three rather than the CSS initial of two: at --leading-relaxed a two-line remainder still reads as a stray.
break-inside: avoid figure, tr A figure is its drawing and its caption. A row is one record.
break-inside: avoid .cf-vacancy, .cf-blog-card, .cf-info-card, .cf-process, .lp-ev-card, .cf-team-grid__item, .cf-culture__row, .cf-accordion__item, .cf-contact__row, .cf-value-row, .cf-subdiv__cell, .cf-stat, .cf-quote, .cf-event, .cf-annot-fig, .cf-plot, .cf-pie, .cf-line, .cf-gantt, .cf-block, .cf-calendar Each is one object in the drawing: one contour around it, or one figure and its caption, or one record.
break-inside: avoid .cf-construct__plate Two drawings registered on one viewBox. A sheet boundary through it does not cut a figure in half, it cuts an object away from the geometry that explains it — and the half that survives is a drawing with rays running off it to nothing.

The register held the drawing and not the card it stands in. .lp-ev-card — act 6's evidence card on the landing page — is a figure and, under it, the eyebrow, the headline and the source list that say where the figure's numbers came from, all inside one border. .cf-pie, .cf-plot, .cf-line and .cf-block were already on the list, and all four of them are the figure inside one of these four cards, so the only sheet boundary the register still left available was the one between a chart and the citation that makes it a claim rather than a picture. Measured on index.html at A4 with the page box above: eleven sheets, with an open contour running off the foot of sheets 7, 8 and 9 and three sets of sources printed overleaf from their charts. The four cards are 691, 745, 677 and 754 px tall against 265 mm — 1002 px — of sheet, so each of them moves whole and the sheet count does not change.

One cut on that page is not this register's to make. Act 3's first .cf-process card is still divided at sheet 2, and it has been on the list since this chapter was written. It is the first in-flow child of the pin's step list, cf-pin__steps, and Blink will not push a box out of its container's first fragment, because that would leave the fragment holding nothing at all; the three cards behind it obey the rule, because by then the container has already put content on the earlier sheet. Giving that list so much as a 1 px ::before in print moves that card whole and leaves the printout with no open contour anywhere — which says what the cause is, and also that the cures are a forced break before the act or a spacer inside it. Both are compositions, and neither is a line this register can carry.

A table is deliberately not on that list. A table is not one object, it is a stack of rows, and it is free to run over as many sheets as it needs — which is only true if it takes its head with it. Otherwise the reader is left holding a grid of numbers with nothing saying which column is which. 

thead { display: table-header-group; }
tfoot { display: table-footer-group; }

That is also why the head row was already given white-space: normal in print by Table: a repeated head is a head that has to fit more than once.

The address of a thing you cannot click

An article's outbound links are its sources, and on a sheet a source with no address is an assertion. This is the one place in the system where CSS writes text that is not in the markup, and it is scoped to the prose of an article for exactly that reason: the address is printed where the reader is being asked to believe something, and nowhere else. 

.cf-prose a[href^="http"]::after {
  content: " (" attr(href) ")";
  font-family: var(--font-mono);
  font-size: var(--text-xs);
  word-break: break-all;
}

Internal links are left alone. Those are the site talking to itself, and a reader holding a sheet of it does not need the route. The mono face is the system's face for machine strings, and an address is one.

Where the rules live, and why in two places

The law and the page box are in base.css. The register of components is at the foot of components.css. That split is a cascade fact and not a second opinion: base.css loads first, so a display: none written there for .cf-nav loses to the display: flex in components.css at equal specificity. Written in the wrong file it is not a decision, it is a comment — which is how the first draft of this chapter shipped a nav bar onto every sheet while claiming to have removed it.

FileHolds
base.css@page, .container, the ground, headings, paragraphs, figure, tr, thead/tfoot, .skip-link, and the statement of the law.
components.cssThe two registers: what is withdrawn, and what may not be cut. Plus the print decisions that were already there beside their own components.
acts.cssThe landing page's own two, which predate this chapter and are unchanged.
docs.cssNothing. This documentation site does not ship, and printing it is not a path anyone is on.

How to check it

Print styles are the one part of this system a screenshot cannot show you, which is most of why they drifted. Two ways to see them, both without printing anything:

  • DevTools → Rendering → Emulate CSS media type: print. The page re-renders in place. This shows every rule but no sheet boundary, so it answers “what is on the page” and not “where is it cut”.
  • Print to PDF and read the sheets. The only way to see fragmentation. Headless works: chrome --headless --print-to-pdf=out.pdf <url>.

Check with Background graphics on at least once. It is the setting a reader turns on when they want a figure to keep its colour, and it is the setting under which the ground used to flood every sheet — the fault stays invisible in the default.

Do and don't

Do

  • Add a new object to the may-not-be-cut register the day you add the component, not the day someone prints it.
  • Give a withdrawn affordance's reservations back on the same line you withdraw it — the hero's three are the worked example.
  • Let a colour distinguish, and let a line distinguish too. If the figure survives forced-colors it survives paper.
  • Write page measurements in mm. A4 and Letter disagree about inches.
  • Put a rule where it can win: the object registers belong at the foot of components.css.

Don't

  • Reach for print-color-adjust: exact. It asks the reader for ink to make up for a drawing that does not work, and it is off by default anyway.
  • Leave an object with no paint but a background. That is how the primary button printed as a floating phrase.
  • Put break-inside: avoid on a container that can legitimately run long — a table, a section, .cf-prose. It does not prevent the break, it just makes the browser start the whole thing on a fresh sheet.
  • Assume vh means what it means on a screen. In a print context it resolves against the page box, which is how one headline came to own 85 % of sheet 1.
  • Hide something on paper because it looks busy. Withdraw it because it cannot be operated, or leave it.
