The Light Layer

The system is six material layers in a fixed order, and five of them have a chapter. Light — layer 5, the one the whole brand is named after in everything but the word — was documented as a property of the palette, a note in the motion chapter, a rung of the found state and a row in four different tables. This is its chapter: what separates a light from a thing that is merely lit, how much of it a screen may carry, and the one place the rule about it had gone stale.

Why this is a layer and not a colour

The stack on the front door draws the six, back to front: the page's own wash, then whatever is opaque, then glass, then the contour that draws it, then light, then the words. Every other layer answers to a chapter that is about it — the wash, materials, glass, geometry, typography. Layer 5 pointed at three chapters, none of which is about light: a section of the colour chapter, the found state, and the line of sight. That is not a documentation gap so much as a category error, and it produced a real one downstream, which the last section of this page is about.

Light is not a colour in this system. It is a source and a falloff — the phrase the colour chapter has always used — and that is why lime never fills an area flat, why the glow shifts hue as it fades rather than fading in alpha, and why the foil is a rake rather than a tint. A colour is a value. A light has a direction, a distance and a place it comes from.

Lime is the layer's colour and is not the layer. Glas and Sky are in the light family too — every ramp here runs out of lime, through Glas, and away to CF-Grau — and the budget below is a budget on sources, not on appearances of a hex value. That distinction is what the rest of this page is for.

A source, and everything else is falloff

The system's oldest rule about light is one lime moment per screen. It is restated on most of the component pages and in every shipping stylesheet, and it is right; taken literally it is also broken by three things that ship, which is how you can tell it was a count standing in for a law. The law is:

One source per screen. A falloff is unbudgeted.

A source is where light comes from: a nucleus, a lit contour, a lit face, a cap, a rim. It is the mark that says this is the place something happens, and a screen with two of them has two places and therefore none. A falloff is a surface catching light that comes from somewhere else — a foil headline, the rim on a sheet of glass, the pool on a lit ground, a black button's label. It carries the same family of ramps and it makes no claim about where the eye should go, so counting it was never the point.

The three things that break the count are the three the system already had to write an exemption for, one at a time, in three different places, and each of them is a falloff:

What shipsWhere it was excusedWhat it actually is
the black button's label, filled with the foil “the button's label is under no budget, because there the foil is the component's colour rather than an emphasis” a falloff. There can be several black buttons on a page and none of them is a place something happens.
a page with twelve matches, all drawn in the found state “the one lime moment is satisfied by the drawing rather than waived for it” one source — the current match — and eleven falloffs. The lit rung is the light; the contoured rung is a mark that has been found.
a drawing with six annotations “a drawing with six notes composes one lit anchor and five contoured ones” the same shape again, at object scale. One anchor is lit and it is the source.

Three separate arguments, all correct, all making the same move, and none of them able to see the other two. Naming the move once turns three exemptions into one rule that generates them — and, more usefully, tells the next component which side it is on before it needs an exemption written for it.

Four sources, one family, and each of them has a place the light comes out of. Beside them, the same family used the other way round:

The two groups are drawn from the same family and the difference between them is not how bright they are. It is whether the mark has an inside the light comes out of. That test decides every case in this system and it decides them the same way a technical illustrator would: you can point at the lamp.

Lime is never flat, and where it is

The rule is the layer's core law and belongs to the colour chapter, which states it, measures it and says which ramp a mark takes. What belongs here is its boundary — the shape of the thing the rule does not reach — because that was written as a list of three names, and the system ships seven flat lime areas. Five of them are the found state's registers, all one kind; the other two had no row at all.

BoundaryWhy the ramp is not available or not meant
A highlight background-image is not painted on a highlight pseudo-element, so there is no gradient form of the declaration to reach for. .cf-mark--current and the calendar's today are elements that could ramp and are held to the same means on purpose: a match is one drawing wherever it is drawn. → The found state

A source's nucleus a light's falloff is the glow it throws, not a ramp inside it. The core is the source; the ramp is around it, and it is there.
Not an area a stroke, an outline, a focus ring. A line has no plane to ramp across, and the rule is about area. This is the rule's edge rather than an exception inside it, so it carries no rows in the census below and is enforced by not being looked at.

The nucleus, measured

The third row is the one that was missing, and it is the interesting one, because the colour chapter's own table two paragraphs above the old list says a rim, a ring, a nucleus → the bloom. The landing page draws twenty-five sources that do not, and they are right. Measured on the rendered page at 1440 × 900:

A ramp inside a 3.6 px core would be a more expensive way to draw a flat dot, and it would be the wrong drawing even if it were free: the falloff is already there, three times the diameter of the thing that is throwing it. The rule was never violated. The exception list was a list of places rather than a statement of the boundary, and a list of places goes stale the first time the same argument turns up at a different address — which is exactly what the legend key on the same page is.

Rules

  • One source per screen. Count sources, not appearances of lime. A screen with two places something happens has none.
  • A falloff is unbudgeted and is still not decoration. It has to report a light that exists in the drawing. A foil headline on a page with no source is a gradient, and the reader has nothing to attribute it to.
  • Wherever it fills an area, it fills it with a ramp — outside the four boundaries above, and the boundaries are a shape rather than a list of addresses. → the rule
  • Light sits above the contour and below the text. That is what makes it lighten a ghost rather than replace it, and it is why the arrival band is capped at 0.18 rather than at whatever still clears contrast.
  • Never as ink. Lime on black measures 18.5:1 and still may not set a letterform: near-white luminance at the palette's highest chroma blooms on a dark ground. Type takes white or the foil. → Lime is light, not ink
  • A source may move its light; it may not move. What answers to the reader is the angle a ramp is drawn at and the position of a mask — never a colour, an opacity or a place. → The line of sight

Three ways to get it wrong

  1. Lighting the important thing. The source is where something happens, not where the most important content is. A lit headline and a lit CTA on one screen are two lamps in one room, and the room goes flat.
  2. Turning a falloff into a source by making it brighter. A foil that has been pushed until it reads as a light is a headline competing with the mark it was supposed to point at. The foil's whole claim is hue travel inside a narrow luminance band; widening the band is how it stops being foil and starts being a fade.
  3. Writing an exemption instead of finding the boundary. Three of them were written before the rule underneath was noticed, and a fourth was needed for the legend key before this page existed. If a mark needs an exemption, the first question is which of the four boundaries it is on.
